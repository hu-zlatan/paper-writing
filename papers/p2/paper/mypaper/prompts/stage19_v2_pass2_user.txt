# Probe, Then Certify: A Tactile Friction-Onset Probe with Conformal Risk Control for Certified-Slip Minimal-Force Grasping in Simulation

## Abstract

A minimal-force grasp should state how often it slips. Non-tactile conformal baselines can, but they are friction-blind: their calibrated force must cover the whole friction range. After a fixed, friction-uninformative preload, our gripper lowers its force until a touch-only detector reports incipient slip, giving a Coulomb friction estimate. A force rule using it (physics plug-in or learned quantile model) is calibrated by conformal risk control (CRC) on the monotonised binary *physical* slip loss, per disturbance stratum, so that expected slip is at most α. This offline certificate needs simulator replays of every calibration grasp; on a robot, binary-feedback tracking is the deployable route. In MuJoCo at α = 0.10, at assumed onset noise σ = 0.2 and friction μ ∈ [0.3, 1.0], the pre-registered comparison against the strongest non-tactile conformal baseline gives D = 0.4168 (97.5 % CI [0.3484, 0.4778]) less relative excess *hold* force, at test slip 0.0829 against 0.1021. D falls to 0.1401 at σ = 0.4, and in a narrow-friction (μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8]), mid-amplitude sub-pool the method is worse (D = −0.2614). Secondary analyses: the saving persists at matched slip and under a per-stratum Learn-then-Test certificate. On one (refined) grid, a plug-in on the same onset reaches D 0.4149 and the learned arm 0.4289; the learned model shows no demonstrated gain over the plug-in (not confirmed). A non-tactile friction prior of log-noise 0.2 leaves no shown advantage. Under shift, frozen calibration breaks while binary-feedback tracking keeps window slip near α; efficiency against oracle recalibration is inconclusive.

## 1 Introduction

Many manipulation tasks call for the smallest *holding* grip force that still keeps the object in the hand through the motions and loads of the task. Sustained excess holding force loads the object and the actuator for the whole hold, and slip prevention with limited force is a recognised concern for prosthetic hands \cite{berman2025autonomous}; too little force drops the object. Humans solve this trade-off by adapting grip force to the friction of the surface they touch \cite{johansson1984roles} and by re-regulating it when friction changes suddenly \cite{willemet2024grip}. Robotic tactile grip regulation has made steady progress on the same trade-off. Controllers detect incipient slip and tighten the grasp \cite{dong2019maintaining, veiga2020grip}, estimate friction online and add a safety margin \cite{khamis2021real, niu2026synchronized}, or learn a map from tactile signals to a gentle grasp force \cite{boonstra2024estimate}. What these controllers do not provide is a quantitative, distribution-free statement of the form "this force rule slips on at most a fraction α of grasps". Margins are set by hand, and their slip rate is whatever the experiment happens to show. We are explicit about which force this paper minimises: the hold force commanded after a short, fixed sensing protocol. That protocol (Section 3.2) applies the same 32.7 N preload and a squeeze to 1.5 times the preload to every object, so peak force during the protocol is not reduced by any method studied here, and objects that cannot tolerate that peak, which includes many fragile objects, are outside the scope of the claims.

Conformal methods supply exactly this kind of statement. Split conformal prediction \cite{lei2017distribution} and conformal risk control \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal} turn any predictor into a rule whose expected loss on an exchangeable test point is bounded by a target level, using a held-out calibration set and no distributional assumptions. In grasping, the predictor proposes a force and calibration inflates it until the empirical slip risk on calibration grasps is at most α. The guarantee is cheap, but its price in force depends on what the predictor knows. A rule that cannot see friction must cover the whole friction range with one inflation factor, and with μ spanning a factor of more than three, the calibrated force is conservative for most objects. The relevant comparison for a tactile method is therefore not an uncalibrated controller but the *strongest non-tactile conformal baseline*: a method that uses everything a robot knows without touch (mass, commanded motion, aperture, even privileged object-family identity) and is calibrated to the same α. We selected this comparator on a disjoint selection split before any calibration or test data were read. The physics plug-in CSF-1 (selection-split mean relative excess 0.7686) beat Mondrian-CSF with privileged family identity (0.8294) and a learned proprioceptive predictor CertGrip-P (0.8335).

This framing raises three questions. Can a ramp-down probe supply enough friction information to cut excess grip force at a certified physical slip rate of at most α? Which component carries the saving: static touch, a probing squeeze, the friction-onset probe, or a learned model on top? And does calibration survive distribution shift when the only feedback available on a robot is whether the object slipped?

Our answer combines one mechanism with one statistical tool. The mechanism is the probe. After a fixed preload F₀ = 32.7 N, identical for every object and therefore uninformative about mass and friction, the gripper lowers its force until a touch-only detector reports incipient slip; under Coulomb friction the onset force gives μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset). The tool is CRC on the binary physical slip loss, applied per disturbance stratum. Its guarantee, which we call "certified" throughout, is marginal and in expectation: within a stratum, the slip rate is at most α in expectation over the calibration draw and the test grasp. We report a high-probability Learn-then-Test variant \cite{angelopoulos2025learn} next to it, whose guarantee also holds per stratum only. "Certified" is a statement about simulation: both offline certificates need, for every calibration grasp, the physical outcome of direct replays of that same grasp at every grid force, which only a simulator can provide. The route that could carry over to a robot is online tracking from observed binary slip; we study it with physical feedback only descriptively, on 5 stream orderings and without intervals, so nothing in this paper is certified on hardware. CRC on the physical loss, rather than split conformal on simulator labels, is needed because the pre-registered monotonicity gate failed: in 8 of 800 instances the label rule was not monotone in force.

**Contributions.**
- **(C1) Method: probe, then a simple force rule, then a certificate.** A grasp protocol in which a friction-free preload makes a ramp-down onset probe the only friction channel, and CRC on the monotonised physical slip loss turns any probe-fed force rule into one whose expected slip is at most α per stratum, in simulation, where the replays it needs exist. The simplest instance is a one-line Coulomb plug-in on the onset; the learned quantile model is optional.
- **(C2) Pre-registered evaluation against the best non-tactile conformal comparator, with its conditions stated.** At α = 0.10, an assumed onset noise σ = 0.2 and friction log-uniform on [0.3, 1.0], drawn independently of every non-tactile input, the probe arm reduces mean relative excess hold force by D = 0.4168 (CI\*\* [0.3484, 0.4778]) relative to CSF-1. The effect is set by those conditions: D is 0.6789 at σ = 0 and 0.1401 at σ = 0.4, where its lower limit falls below the smallest effect of interest, and it reverses (D = −0.2614) in a sub-pool with μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8] and mid-range disturbance amplitudes. Secondary analyses compare the arms at matched slip and under a high-probability certificate.
- **(C3) Attribution.** Ablations and stronger baselines locate the friction information in the probe. A physics plug-in on the same onset is superior to CSF-1 on its own (D 0.4149 against the learned arm's 0.4289 on the same refined grid; 0.3816 against 0.4168 on the primary grid, where the plug-in is over-protected by a grid floor). The learned model shows no demonstrated gain over that plug-in at the primary noise level (not confirmed; no equivalence test was registered); static touch and the squeeze add no demonstrated gain; a sufficiently good non-tactile friction prior leaves no shown advantage; and there is a sub-pool in which the method loses.
- **(C4) Online calibration under shift from binary feedback.** Frozen calibration breaks under friction and amplitude shifts, while quantile tracking keeps slip near α, also with physical feedback (descriptive). Efficiency against oracle recalibration is inconclusive.

We state the confirmatory record plainly. Of four pre-registered core decisions one is confirmed (the main comparison); the validity premise and the learned-versus-plug-in contrast are not confirmed, and the online efficiency decision is inconclusive. ## 2 Related Work

**Tactile slip, friction reading and grip control.** Reading friction from incipient slip is an established idea. Rubber-based tactile sensors were used early on to detect incipient slip and control grip \cite{marconi1996incipient}, and surveys of tactile friction estimation and slip detection \cite{chen2018tactile, romeo2020methods} describe a mature literature in which incipient slip, the partial slip at the contact periphery that precedes gross sliding, is the key friction cue. Human precision grip adapts force to surface friction \cite{johansson1984roles} and to sudden friction changes \cite{willemet2024grip}. On the robot side, slip detectors on multifingered hands \cite{james2020slip}, learned temporal detectors on barometric sensors \cite{grover2022detect} and reactive multifingered controllers that combine slip onset with internal-force optimisation \cite{ayral2026reactive} trigger corrective tightening. Closer to minimal-force grasping, \cite{dong2019maintaining} keep grasps within slipping bounds by monitoring incipient slip, \cite{khamis2021real} estimate friction in real time and add a margin to the target force, \cite{boonstra2024estimate} learn to estimate incipient slip for gentle grasping, and \cite{niu2026synchronized} couple online friction estimation with adaptive grasp control. Learned and reflex controllers extend this to dynamic interaction \cite{kuo2025tracing}, vision-tactile reflexes \cite{feng2026tactilereflex} and sim-to-real force policies \cite{lach2024zero}; adaptive analytical controllers come with Lyapunov arguments \cite{almohammed2022switched}, independent finger controllers stabilise grasps \cite{veiga2020grip}, and vision-language models select pre-grasp forces from experience \cite{shang2026exp}. In all of these the margin is reactive, hand-set or estimated, and none states a distribution-free bound on the resulting slip rate. Two families from this list are the natural tactile competitors of our method: reactive controllers that detect slip during the hold and tighten, and controllers that estimate friction and add a tuned safety factor. We do not evaluate either (Section 7); our comparisons are against non-tactile conformal rules, a physics plug-in on our own probe and an uncalibrated fixed margin on that plug-in, so the paper does not show that probe plus CRC beats existing tactile grip control at equal slip.

**Robust and safe grasping under uncertainty.** Model-based approaches bound the risk of losing a grasp through assumed uncertainty sets. Grasping-force optimisation has been examined under parameter uncertainty \cite{cloutier2018examining}; control barrier functions enforce friction-cone constraints \cite{cortez2019control} and, with disturbance observers and tactile sensing, give adaptive safe grasping \cite{kim2024robust}; friction-informed risk margins \cite{enwerem2026firmgrasp} and variational belief parameterisations \cite{enwerem2026variational} make grasp synthesis tolerant of multimodal uncertainty. These guarantees hold relative to a model and its uncertainty set. Our certificate holds relative to the data-generating distribution of grasps, without a friction model, at the price of being marginal over grasps and needing calibration data from that distribution.

**Conformal prediction and risk control.** Conformal prediction gives finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees under exchangeability \cite{vovk2005algorithmic, lei2017distribution, angelopoulos2023conformal}. Conformalized quantile regression adapts to the input \cite{romano2019conformalized}; training-conditional \cite{bian2023training} and class-conditional or clustered \cite{ding2023class} variants refine the marginal statement. Conformal risk control generalises coverage to bounded monotone losses \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal}, with extensions to non-monotone losses \cite{angelopoulos2026conformal} and non-exchangeable data \cite{farinhas2023non}; Learn-then-Test gives high-probability risk control by multiple testing \cite{angelopoulos2025learn}. Under shift, adaptive conformal inference \cite{gibbs2021adaptive, gibbs2022conformal}, conformal PID control and quantile tracking \cite{angelopoulos2023conformala}, decaying step sizes \cite{angelopoulos2024online} and semi-bandit feedback \cite{ge2024stochastic} keep long-run coverage near target. In robotics, conformal tools certify decisions \cite{lekeufack2024conformal}, verification and control \cite{lindemann2025formal, lindemann2023conformal}, motion planning among dynamic agents \cite{dixit2022adaptive}, contact-aware state estimation \cite{marques2026particle}, LLM planners \cite{ren2023robots}, sensorimotor policies under shift \cite{huang2023conformal}, RL safety filters \cite{strawn2023conformal}, safe Bayesian optimisation \cite{zhang2024bayesian}, interactive imitation learning \cite{zhao2024conformalized} and perception-planning pipelines \cite{kumar2026learnable}. The controlled quantity there is a prediction set, a trajectory region or a decision loss; none certifies a physical contact outcome as a binary loss over a family of grip forces.

**Novelty.** Lowering the force until slip to read friction is not new. What is new is (i) a protocol in which the onset is the *only* friction channel, so its value can be isolated; (ii) a distribution-free, per-stratum bound on the *physical* slip rate of the resulting minimal hold force, valid in simulation where calibration replays exist; and (iii) evidence that the friction information behind the calibrated saving comes from the probe, with no demonstrated gain from the learned model on top of a plug-in. The statistical tools themselves (CRC, Learn-then-Test, score-space quantile tracking) are used as published; the contribution is the protocol that isolates the friction channel and the attribution it permits.

## 3 Problem Formulation and Method

### 3.1 Certified minimal-force grasping

A grasp instance x comprises an object (family, geometry, mass m, friction coefficient μ, centre-of-mass offset), a grasp pose offset, and a disturbance of known type d ∈ {D1, D2, D3, D4} (lift acceleration, shake, lateral pull, payload added mid-hold) with an amplitude. A parallel-jaw gripper commands a grip force F, and slip(F, x) ∈ {0, 1} is the physical outcome of a full episode: a slip is a tangential displacement of the object relative to the pad frame above 2 mm, a rotation about the grasp axis above 3°, or loss of pad contact. The minimal no-slip force F\*(x) is bracketed in simulation by log-space bisection on [F_min, F_max] = [0.1, 100] N with one replay at F_max and 10 halvings (11 replays), giving F\*_lo < F\*_hi and F\*_mid = √(F\*_lo F\*_hi). A force rule is scored by its mean relative excess and its physical slip rate,

$$\mathrm{RE}(F) = \mathbb{E}_x\big[(F(x) - F^*_{\mathrm{mid}}(x))/F^*_{\mathrm{mid}}(x)\big], \qquad \mathrm{SR}(F) = \mathbb{E}_x[\mathrm{slip}(F(x), x)],$$

where RE is signed and includes slipped instances. RE scores only the hold force F(x) chosen after the sensing protocol of Section 3.2; the protocol's own forces are identical for every method and are not part of RE. RE is computed over avoidable instances, those with F\*_hi ≤ F_max; an unavoidable instance, which no admissible force holds, is excluded from RE and counts as a slip at every force in every risk estimate. The task is

$$\min_F \ \mathrm{RE}(F) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathrm{SR}(F) \le \alpha,$$

with the constraint enforced without a contact model, from calibration instances drawn from the test distribution. Two rules A and B are compared on identical test instances by $D = 1 - \mathrm{RE}(A)/\mathrm{RE}(B)$, so D > 0 means that A needs less excess force. The labels F\* exist only in simulation; this is why the certificate below is defined on the physical outcome and why Section 3.5 gives a label-free online route.

### 3.2 Grasp protocol and the friction-onset probe

Every method runs the same protocol, so every method sees the same physical history up to the moment it chooses F. The gripper closes to a constant preload F₀ = 32.7 N (m g/μ with m_max = 1 kg and μ_min = 0.3) and holds it for 0.4 s; because F₀ is the same for every instance, it encodes neither mass nor friction. A probe squeeze F₀ → 1.5 F₀ → F₀ follows over 0.3 s. Then comes the **ramp-down probe**: the force falls log-linearly from F₀ towards 0.005 F₀ over 0.25 s. A detector that reads only the pad touch grids computes the shear/normal ratio r = Σ_pads |shear| / Σ normal from noisy taxels and declares onset when the smoothed log r stops rising (constants in Table 5); a total normal reading below 1 mN declares onset directly. At onset the force returns to F₀ within 0.02 s and holds for 0.1 s; the gripper then ramps to the chosen F, settles, and runs the 0.6 s disturbance window. The slip reference is the pose before the probe, so a slip during the probe counts as a slip of the grasp. The force history up to this point is the same for every object and every method: F₀ for 0.4 s, a peak of 1.5 F₀ in the squeeze, then the descent below F₀ and the return. The hold force F is the only force in which methods differ, and the only one that "minimal force" refers to in this paper.

The onset gives the Coulomb reading μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset), clipped to [0.1, 3]. Because simulated touch is cleaner than a real sensor, we add onset noise in log units, $\tilde f = \log F_{\mathrm{onset}} + \sigma\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ drawn once per instance and σ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4}. All core decisions use **σ = 0.2**, an assumed level; σ = 0 is an upper bound on what the probe can deliver. The same noisy onset is passed to every arm that uses it. Static touch at the preload hold and the squeeze features are recorded from noise-free grids, which favours the arms that use them and makes the attribution conservative.

### 3.3 Force rules

Every rule has the form $F(x;\lambda) = \min\big(F_{\max},\, F_{\mathrm{base}}(x)\, e^{\lambda}\big)$, where λ is a log-force offset set by calibration. They differ only in $F_{\mathrm{base}}$ and in what it may read (Table 4).

- **Probe-fed plug-in CSF-μ̂:** $F_{\mathrm{base}} = m(g + a_{\mathrm{cmd}})/(2\hat\mu)$, with μ̂ from the noisy onset. This is the simplest instance of the method.
- **Probe-fed learned model:** a gradient-boosted quantile predictor ĝ(x) at level τ = 1 − α. Its nested feature sets are P (proprioception: disturbance type, commanded acceleration, log mass, aperture at contact) ⊂ S (+ static touch and a preload-slip flag) ⊂ full (+ squeeze features) ⊂ CertGrip-full (+ onset feature $(\tilde f, \text{found})$); onset-only is P plus the onset feature.
- **Non-tactile baselines:** CSF-1, $F_{\mathrm{base}} = m(g + a_{\mathrm{cmd}})/(2\bar\mu)$ for D1/D2 and $m g/(2\bar\mu)$ for D3/D4, with $\bar\mu$ the geometric mean of the μ range; Mondrian-CSF, the same formula with one offset per family × disturbance cell (16 cells) \cite{ding2023class}, given privileged family identity; and CertGrip-P. The comparator COMP is the best of these three by mean label-rule RE at α = 0.10 on the selection split, committed before any calibration or test file was read.
- **CSF-proxy (second round):** the CSF-1 formula with $\bar\mu$ replaced by a non-tactile friction prior $\tilde\mu = \mathrm{clip}(\mu\, e^{\sigma_p \varepsilon_p}, 0.1, 3.0)$, $\varepsilon_p \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ per instance and independent of ε, σ_p ∈ {0.2, 0.4, 0.8}. It is an oracle-side construction that asks how good a friction prior from material or vision would have to be; it is not a deployable estimator. σ_p is log-noise on a friction prior and σ is log-noise on an onset force; the two are different noise models and are never placed on one scale.
- **Probe + fixed margin (second round, uncalibrated):** $F = (1 + \kappa)\, F_{\mathrm{base}}^{\hat\mu}$, the uncalibrated CSF-μ̂ force with one hand-set margin κ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0}.

Mass is given to every method on the stated assumption that a robot can weigh the object during the lift; external disturbance amplitudes (D3, D4) are hidden from all methods. In the simulated protocol the hold force is chosen before any lift, so supplying the mass is an assumption about available information, not a step we model.

### 3.4 Certificates on the physical binary loss

For an arm with base force $F_{\mathrm{base}}$ and calibration instance i, define the physical loss and its monotonised version on a grid Λ,

$$L_i(\lambda) = \mathbb{1}\{\text{slip in a direct replay at } F(x_i;\lambda)\}, \qquad \tilde L_i(\lambda) = \max_{\lambda' \ge \lambda,\ \lambda' \in \Lambda} L_i(\lambda').$$

The loss is the outcome of a fresh simulated episode, not the bisection label. $\tilde L_i$ is non-increasing in λ and $\tilde L_i \ge L_i$, so a risk bound on $\tilde L$ implies the same bound on physical slip.

**In-expectation certificate (CRC, primary).** Within each stratum, with n = 500 calibration instances and $\tilde R_n(\lambda) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \tilde L_i(\lambda)$,

$$\hat\lambda = \min\Big\{\lambda \in \Lambda : \frac{n \tilde R_n(\lambda) + 1}{n + 1} \le \alpha\Big\},$$

and λ̂ = max Λ if no grid point qualifies, a fallback that voids the bound and is reported. By \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal}, a test instance exchangeable with the stratum's calibration instances satisfies E[slip at F(x; λ̂)] ≤ α. The expectation runs over the calibration set and the test instance together: the bound is marginal and per stratum, not a guarantee for one deployment, one object or one family.

**High-probability certificate (Learn-then-Test, secondary).** Per stratum, λ is scanned from the top of the grid downwards and kept while the one-sided Clopper–Pearson upper bound at δ = 0.1 of the calibration physical risk at λ is at most α; λ̂_LTT is the last kept value (max Λ, flagged, if none) \cite{angelopoulos2025learn}. This gives P(risk ≤ α) ≥ 1 − δ per stratum over the calibration draw, the training-conditional view of \cite{bian2023training}. It holds per stratum at δ = 0.1, not family-wise across strata: the probability that at least one of the four strata exceeds α is bounded only by 4δ under a union bound, and we did not rerun LTT at δ/4.

**Where the certificates apply.** Both certificates need $L_i(\lambda)$ on the whole grid, that is, the physical outcome of the *same* calibration grasp replayed at every grid force. A simulator provides this exactly; a robot cannot re-run a physically identical grasp 20 times. The offline certificates are therefore statements about the simulated grasp distribution, and Section 3.5 gives the route that needs only one observed outcome per grasp.

**Grids.** The primary CRC grid is fixed per arm before calibration: Λ = q̂_sel + linspace(−0.4, 0.4, 20), with q̂_sel the label-rule conformal quantile of the arm's scores on the selection split, pooled over strata. A post hoc refined grid, q̂_sel,d + linspace(−0.8, 0.8, 41) per stratum, is a sensitivity route; under its pre-fixed reporting rule it can mark a primary-grid result as unreliable but cannot upgrade a decision. For the second-round CSF-proxy arms a grid-edge rule was registered: if a stratum's λ̂ lands on index 0 or 19, the arm is re-run on the refined-grid recipe.

```
Algorithm 1: probe-then-certify (per arm, per disturbance stratum d)
input : base-force rule F_base (CSF-mu-hat plug-in or learned model), grid Lambda,
        calibration instances {x_i} in stratum d, level alpha
0  every instance: preload F0, squeeze, ramp-down probe -> F_onset (+ onset noise sigma); F_base(x) uses it
1  for i in calibration set, for lambda in Lambda:
2       L[i, lambda] <- slip of a direct replay at min(F_max, F_base(x_i) * exp(lambda))
3  Ltilde[i, lambda] <- max_{lambda' >= lambda} L[i, lambda']                       # monotonise
4  CRC: lambda_hat <- first lambda with (sum_i Ltilde[i, lambda] + 1)/(n + 1) <= alpha, else max(Lambda) (flag)
5  LTT: scan lambda downwards from max(Lambda); keep while CP_upper(risk at lambda; delta) <= alpha
6  deploy: preload, squeeze, probe -> F_onset; command F = min(F_max, F_base(x) * exp(lambda_hat))
```

**Design rationale.** The pre-registered certificate was split conformal on bisection labels with the one-sided score $s = \log F^*_{\mathrm{hi}} - \log \hat g(x)$ \cite{romano2019conformalized}. It certifies the label event F < F\*_hi, which transfers to physical slip only if slip is monotone in F. The monotonicity gate tested this on 800 instances and failed (8 non-monotone, one-sided Clopper–Pearson upper limit 0.02115 > 0.02; 7 of 8 under D2). Following the registered failure branch, the primary route became CRC on the physical loss, which needs no monotonicity; the label route is reported as secondary. The monotonised loss costs slight conservatism wherever the raw loss flickers. LTT is added because CRC's in-expectation bound permits realised slip above α on a given calibration set.

**Complexity.** Calibration needs |Λ| direct replays of the same instance (20 on the primary grid), which only a simulator can supply; choosing λ̂ is O(|Λ| n) per stratum, and LTT adds one Clopper–Pearson bound per grid point. Because every calibration and test instance is replayed on the full grid, the bootstrap recomputes λ̂ in each draw without new simulation. At deployment the method costs one probe of under 0.4 s and a closed-form force.

### 3.5 Online calibration from binary feedback

On hardware, F\* labels and replays are unavailable, but whether the object slipped is observed after every grasp. Score-space online quantile tracking (OQT) \cite{angelopoulos2023conformala} updates one log-force offset per stratum,

$$q_{t+1} = q_t + \eta\,(\mathrm{slip}_t - \alpha), \qquad F_t = \min\big(F_{\max},\, F_{\mathrm{base}}(x_t)\, e^{q_t}\big),$$

with η = 0.1 × IQR of the in-distribution calibration scores (η = 0.03412, frozen) and anti-windup that skips the update when F_t was capped at F_max. We track in score space rather than with the level update of adaptive conformal inference \cite{gibbs2021adaptive}, because a friction drop can move scores beyond the support of the calibration scores, where a level-indexed tracker saturates. Reference arms are decaying-step OQT \cite{angelopoulos2024online}, frozen offline calibration, and an oracle that recalibrates on labelled instances from the shifted distribution. Two feedback variants are studied. The pre-registered study uses label-rule feedback slip_t = 1{F_t < F\*_hi,t} with the label-route offset as warm start. The second-round study feeds the physical slip of a replay at F_t and warm-starts from the CRC λ̂, which is the route a robot would use. The guarantee of the tracker is of a different kind from CRC: with the update above, the long-run average slip over uncapped steps approaches α by an accounting identity, whatever the shift, but no statement is made about any single window or about efficiency. "Keeps slip near α" is therefore expected behaviour rather than a finding; what the experiments measure is how much force the tracker needs to do so, how fast it recovers after a shift, and how it compares with frozen and oracle calibration.

## 4 Experimental Setup

**Simulator and objects.** All experiments run in MuJoCo 4.0.0 (timestep 0.002 s, elliptic friction cone, impratio 10, condim 6), with physics parameters identical for every family and shift. The parallel-jaw gripper has a force-controlled closing axis and one touch grid per pad. Fast tactile simulators exist \cite{wang2022tacto, akinola2025tacsl, leins2025hydroelastictouch}, and sensor configuration matters for learned manipulation \cite{luu2025manifeel, birtalan2026impact}; we use MuJoCo's pad grids with the explicit noise model of Section 3.2. In-distribution families are F1 boxes, F2 cylinders, F3 capsules and F4 off-CoM composites; F5 thin plates are held out as a new-family shift. Friction is log-uniform on [0.3, 1.0] and mass log-uniform on [0.05, 1.0] kg. Friction is drawn independently of family, geometry, mass, CoM offset and disturbance, so no non-tactile input carries friction information by construction. Disturbances are applied with the hand held still: D1 lift acceleration U[1, 10] m/s² and D2 shake (amplitude U[5, 20] mm at U[2, 5] Hz) enter as inertial pseudo-forces, D3 lateral pull U[0.5, 2]·m g and D4 payload U[0.2, 1.0]·m as forces on the object. Every object receives one instance per disturbance type. A report-only check is consistent with static touch carrying no friction information in this contact model: the summed pad normal reading differs from F₀ by at most 0.1597 N; the summed pad shear reading is close to m g/2 for almost all instances (99th-percentile relative deviation 0.0003) but deviates by up to 6.40 N in the worst case, with 24 instances more than 1 % away; and no instance slipped during the preload. Static touch is not uninformative about everything: it identifies the object family well above chance (Section 5.2).

**Splits.** Splits are by object within family: train objects 0–149, selection 150–199, calibration 200–324, test 325–499. Calibration has n = 500 instances per stratum (2,000 in total) and test has 2,800 (700 per stratum). The selection split (800 instances from 200 objects) is used only to choose COMP and to centre the grids. Pilot objects, used to set the detector constants, are disjoint from all of these.

**Shifts and streams.** S1 lowers friction (μ × 0.6), S1m lowers it mildly (μ × 0.9, not a primary shift), S3 scales every disturbance amplitude by 1.5, and S2 introduces family F5. The pre-registered streams have T = 2,000 steps: steps 1–500 in-distribution, the shift from step 501, evaluation window 1,001–2,000, 20 orderings, label-rule feedback, with physical outcomes audited by direct replay on ordering 0. The second-round physical-feedback streams use the same pools and orderings 0–4 for S1 and S3 only, with CertGrip-full and CSF-1, 500 in-distribution steps followed by 1,500 shifted steps, and windows 1–500 (pre-shift), 1,001–2,000 and 1,501–2,000.

**Metrics and decision rules.** Efficiency is RE against F\*_mid with D as the effect size; validity is the physical slip rate in direct replay. The four core decisions share a Bonferroni split of a 5 % family-wise level (one-sided 0.0125 each). CI\*\* denotes a two-sided 97.5 % percentile interval from 2,000 object-cluster bootstrap draws that resample calibration and test objects within family and recompute λ̂ (or q̂) in every draw. The main comparison requires D ≥ 0.10 (the smallest effect size of interest, SESOI), a CI\*\* lower limit above 0, and both again with F\*_lo and F\*_hi in place of F\*_mid. The learned-versus-plug-in contrast declares superiority by the same rule and equivalence if the CI\*\* lies inside ±0.10. The online decision passes iff the CI\*\* upper limit of R_E = RE_window(OQT-CertGrip)/RE_window(Oracle-CertGrip) is below 1.10 in S1, S2 and S3 and the two arms' window physical slip rates differ by at most 0.02. σ\* is the smallest σ at which the CI\*\* lower limit of the main D falls below 0.10. Secondary analyses, including all second-round rows, are unadjusted and labelled: the hypothesis document registered Holm's correction within secondary families, the operational addenda replaced it with unadjusted intervals, and we treat this as a deviation (Appendix B) and report no Holm-adjusted values, so individual secondary "superiority" labels carry no family-wise error control; second-round decisions are superiority if the CI\*\* lower limit is above 0, inferiority if the upper limit is below 0, and "not shown" otherwise. Matched-slip RE uses one target slip (0.10), found by linear interpolation between adjacent grid offsets k ∈ {−4, …, +4} and repeated inside every bootstrap draw; all matched-slip intervals have 2,000 of 2,000 valid draws. The offset at which an arm reaches slip 0.10 is read off the *test* set, so D_match is a descriptive, test-selected comparison of two curves, not an operating point a user could choose or a certificate. Every secondary number is labelled with its route: "CRC" (physical loss, grid named) or "label route" (split conformal on bisection labels, whose monotonicity premise failed; Section 5.5). A few diagnostics use other intervals (95 % with 500 draws for the friction-residual diagnostic; a 95 % random-effects interval for the pooled cell analysis; Clopper–Pearson for the monotonicity gate), and we say so where they appear.

### 5.1 Main comparison and the conditions it depends on

**At an assumed onset noise of σ = 0.2, the probe arm needs about 42 % less relative excess hold force than the strongest non-tactile conformal baseline (pre-registered, confirmed).** COMP selection returned CSF-1. ### 5.3 Stronger baselines

**A good enough non-tactile friction prior leaves no shown advantage (held as predicted, secondary, CRC).** As registered, D(CertGrip-full vs CSF-proxy) is 0.8361 [0.8137, 0.8573] at σ_p = 0.8 and 0.5042 [0.4362, 0.5635] at σ_p = 0.4 (primary grid), and at σ_p = 0.2 it is −0.0299 [−0.1903, 0.0816] on the edge-rule grid (primary-grid row 0.0514 [−0.0673, 0.1660], superseded because λ̂ sat at index 0 in D1/D2). The interval first includes 0 at σ_p = 0.2: against a non-tactile prior of log-noise 0.2, CertGrip shows no efficiency advantage, and no equivalence test was registered. The proxy arms' RE is 2.786 [2.527, 3.070] at σ_p 0.8 and 0.9213 [0.8423, 1.002] at σ_p 0.4, against 0.7832 for CSF-1, and 0.4312 [0.3906, 0.4759] at σ_p 0.2 (slip 0.0825) against CertGrip-full's 0.4442 [0.4064, 0.4973] (slip 0.0900) on the same edge-rule grid. Proxy-versus-CSF-1 comparisons were not registered.

**A single uniform margin on the plug-in misses α in opposite directions per stratum (descriptive, weak comparison).** With one hand-set margin κ on the uncalibrated CSF-μ̂ force, no κ in {0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0} brings overall test slip near α (0.9582 at κ = 0 to 0.3350 at κ = 1.0), and at κ = 1.0 the inertial strata are over-protected while D3 and D4 slip at 0.6571 and 0.6186 (Appendix F). This is a weak baseline by construction: the plug-in force for D3/D4 cannot see the hidden external load, so no uniform margin can fit both stratum types. It shows that stratum-specific offsets are needed for this force rule; it is not evidence that calibration is necessary in general, and a per-stratum tuned margin, the obvious practitioner alternative, was not evaluated.

### 5.4 Where it fails

In a sub-pool with μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8] and disturbance amplitudes in the middle half of each type's range (n_cal 352, n_test 548), recalibrated inside the sub-pool, the probe arm is worse than CSF-1: D = −0.2614 [−0.5320, −0.0325] (label route). This secondary analysis carried no registered prediction and would be fragile under a multiplicity correction (its upper limit is close to 0). A plausible reading is that a narrow friction range removes the variation the probe measures while its noise remains; whether the narrow range causes the reversal is not tested (Section 7). The centre-of-mass mechanism test is inconclusive (D_F4 − D_F1 = 0.0342 [−0.0424, 0.1138], label route).

### 5.5 Validity of the certificate

The validity premise is not confirmed. The monotonicity gate found 8 non-monotone instances of 800 (one-sided Clopper–Pearson upper limit 0.02115 > 0.02), 7 of them under shake; the violations are small, the largest slip force above F\*_hi exceeding it by a relative 0.00225 in distribution and 0.00904 in the shift pools. The physical audit of the label route passes for all four arm × α combinations: the label rule slightly over-counts slips (physical minus label Δ between −0.00429 and −0.00143), and direct-replay physical slip of the label-route rules at α 0.10 is 0.1014 [0.0864, 0.1168] for CertGrip-full and 0.1114 [0.0911, 0.1336] for CSF-1. The split-conformal unit test passes 160 of 160 cells (140 of 140 informative).

**Uncalibrated targeters, including a validation-tuned one (secondary, label slip).** Three uncalibrated targeters with CertGrip-full features were registered. The two without any tuning, the raw quantile model and a Gaussian model, over-slip at every level (0.0096–0.0507 above α, every CI\*\* above 0), but at α 0.05 their intervals ([0.020, 0.045] and [0.016, 0.042]) do not exclude the registered +0.025 margin. The third, RAW-Qv, adds one constant shift tuned so that slip on the selection split equals α, pooled over strata and with no finite-sample correction. Its slip exceeds α by 0.0029, 0.0161, 0.0125 and 0.0396 at α = 0.01, 0.05, 0.10 and 0.20, and every CI\*\* contains 0 (at α 0.10: +0.0125 [−0.0222, 0.0586]). The corresponding secondary hypothesis is not confirmed. In distribution, then, a practitioner's validation-tuned shift already lands near α. What CRC adds is a finite-sample, distribution-free statement with the +1 correction and one offset per disturbance stratum, which matters where strata differ (Section 5.3); it does not add a large in-distribution correction to a well-tuned shift. Frozen RAW-Qv was not evaluated under shift.

## 6 Discussion

**What the evidence licenses.** The friction information comes from the probe, and CRC makes the resulting saving certifiable in simulation. A quarter-second ramp-down, read by a touch-only detector and fed into a one-line Coulomb formula, is on its own superior to the strongest non-tactile conformal baseline. Compared on the same grid, the plug-in and the learned arm are close: D 0.4149 against 0.4289 on the refined grid and 0.3816 against 0.4168 on the primary grid, where the plug-in is over-protected by the grid floor. The decision-bearing contrast between them is not confirmed: at σ = 0.2 the learned model shows no demonstrated gain over the plug-in, and since no equivalence test was registered we do not claim that the two are equivalent. In secondary, unadjusted analyses the learner is better at σ 0.4 and worse at σ 0. One exploratory reading is that a learner pays off only when the friction reading is noisy enough that other cues help to denoise it. The friction-residual diagnostic is consistent with a related reading that we did not test: at σ = 0.2 the learned arm's force residual retains more dependence on log μ than the plug-in's (cross-validated R² 0.2142 against 0.0054), as if the learner partly shrank the noisy onset towards the mean. The operative method is therefore *probe + simple force rule + CRC*, with the learner as an option whose value depends on the sensor and is not established here.

**The headline is conditional.** The 42 % figure holds at an assumed σ = 0.2 and a friction range of [0.3, 1.0] that no non-tactile input can predict. D is 0.6789 without added noise, 0.1401 at σ = 0.4, and negative in the narrow-friction sub-pool. Two ingredients set it: how much friction varies across the objects a robot meets, and how precisely its sensor reads the onset. Neither has been measured for a physical system in this work, so the figure should be read as one point on a curve rather than as a property of tactile grip regulation.

**What the second-round analyses add.** Two objections to the first-round result were that the arms realised different slip rates and that the certificate held only in expectation. The frontier addresses the first descriptively: at a common test slip of 0.10, a level located on the test set, the saving is at least as large as at the CRC operating points. The Learn-then-Test variant addresses the second: a per-stratum, high-probability bound at δ = 0.1 costs both arms some force and leaves D near 0.40, though its family-wise level across the four strata is weaker than δ. Both re-analyse the same replays, so they show that the conclusion does not hinge on the choice of certificate, not that it replicates. The probe-plus-fixed-margin study is a weak comparison: a uniform margin cannot see the hidden D3/D4 loads, so its failure says that this force rule needs stratum-specific offsets, not that calibration beats every hand-tuned scheme. The validation-tuned targeter RAW-Qv makes the same point from the other side: in distribution, one tuned shift already lands near α, so what CRC contributes here is a finite-sample, distribution-free statement and per-stratum offsets rather than a large correction.

**How good must a non-tactile prior be?** The CSF-proxy sweep held as predicted: the advantage shrinks as the friction prior improves, and at a log-noise of 0.2 no advantage is shown. We read this as a statement about where the probe's value lies, not as a defeat: the probe obtains its friction information from touch alone, on every grasp, without any prior knowledge of the object's material, and its advantage over a friction prior disappears once that prior is good enough. Because σ_p and σ are different noise models, the sweep does not say that the probe's own estimate has log-noise 0.2. Where vision or object category already predicts friction that well, the probe's marginal value would be small; we did not evaluate a deployable prior of this kind.

**Unexpected results and prior work.** The monotonicity premise failed narrowly, mostly under shake, which forced the physical-loss route. The narrow-friction sub-pool reverses the result, so practitioners should expect the advantage to scale with the friction spread of their object set. Lateral-pull cells were consistently the weakest; one candidate explanation, not tested, is that the hidden D3 amplitude enters the required force as a load multiplier that only a stratum-level offset can absorb, which leaves less room for friction information to help within the stratum. Relative to friction-estimating controllers \cite{khamis2021real, niu2026synchronized} and incipient-slip methods \cite{dong2019maintaining, boonstra2024estimate}, which leave the margin as a design parameter, our results suggest that the same cue plus a model-free calibration step yields a large saving over friction-blind rules, and that learned tactile components should be compared against a calibrated plug-in on the same cue. Whether probe plus CRC beats those controllers at equal slip is not shown, because they were not run. The online results are in line with quantile-tracking theory \cite{angelopoulos2023conformala} and the slower adaptation of decaying steps \cite{angelopoulos2024online}.

**Practical implications.** The probe adds well under a second per grasp: 0.25 s of descent, a return within 0.02 s and a 0.1 s hold, after the 0.4 s preload and 0.3 s squeeze that every arm shares. It also drives every object to incipient slip on every grasp. In simulation it found an onset on every in-distribution instance and displaced the object by a median 0.126 mm at onset (95th percentile 0.2543 mm, maximum 1.92 mm); no instance reached the 2 mm slip threshold during the probe, but the worst case came close. Objects for which sub-millimetre slip, a repeated stick–slip transition on their surface, or the 1.5 F₀ squeeze is unacceptable are outside its scope; the force saving reported here concerns the subsequent hold. Because the offline certificate needs labels and replays that only a simulator provides, the deployable route is the online tracker warm-started from a simulation-calibrated offset and updated from observed slips; it commits the robot to about α T slips over T grasps plus a catch-up transient after a shift, and its efficiency on hardware is unknown.

## 7 Limitations

*Simulation and sensing.* All results come from one simulator (MuJoCo) with rigid contact, rigid primitives and five object families, and a modelled tactile detector. σ = 0.2 is an assumed noise level added on top of the detector's intrinsic error, not a measured property of a real sensor; the main effect weakens quickly with noise, and its lower limit falls below the SESOI at σ = 0.4 (D 0.1401). Hardware grounding of σ was not done. Static touch and the squeeze carry no friction information by construction in this contact model, so that part of the attribution is a simulator consistency check, not a finding about elastomer sensors. Disturbances are body forces applied with the hand held still, not robot motions, and the pads share the object's friction coefficient.

*Instance distribution and baselines.* Friction is drawn independently of all non-tactile inputs, so the ≈ 42 % figure is tied to this distribution. CSF-proxy is an oracle-side construction; no correlated-μ pool, deployable vision or category prior, reactive slip-tightening controller, or friction-estimate controller with a tuned per-stratum margin was evaluated, so the comparison with existing tactile grip control at equal slip is open. The probe-plus-fixed-margin study is a weak, descriptive stand-in for such controllers. Mass is supplied to every method before any lift, which the simulated protocol assumes rather than models.

*Certificate.* F\* labels exist only in simulation, so the offline certificate is not deployable as is; online tracking with physical feedback is the hardware route, and it was studied on 5 orderings, S1 and S3 only, without intervals. The validity premise failed; validity rests on the CRC route adopted after that failure, and the grid fix is post hoc. Certificates are marginal per stratum; Learn-then-Test holds per stratum at δ = 0.1, not family-wise (a union bound gives only 4δ across the four strata), and nothing is guaranteed per object, per family or on hardware. Conditional slip, per family × stratum cell, per friction quintile or per object, was not tabulated, so how unevenly the marginal bound is spread is unknown. Matched-slip comparisons locate the slip level on the test set and are descriptive.

*Force accounting.* The protocol applies the 32.7 N preload and a 1.5 F₀ squeeze to every object before the hold, and RE scores only the hold force. Peak and integrated force over the whole protocol were not reported per arm, and no adaptive-preload variant was run; the minimal-force claim does not extend to peak force, and the probe itself produces micro-slip on every grasp.

*Unresolved contrasts.* The learned-versus-plug-in contrast is unresolved, and no direct CertGrip-full versus CSF-μ̂ contrast exists at matched slip or under Learn-then-Test. The online efficiency decision was underpowered, and its 20-ordering feedback is the label rule with a label-route warm start. The narrow-friction sub-pool is a regime in which the method loses; its cause, the concentration of non-monotone instances under shake and the weak lateral-pull cells are untested, and the exploratory stratifications of the sub-pool loss by detector error and family and of the lateral-pull margins by hidden amplitude were not run. All secondary analyses, including every second-round row, are unadjusted for multiplicity: Holm's correction within secondary families was registered and then dropped by the operational addenda (Appendix B), and several secondary superiority labels and the sub-pool reversal could change under it.

## 8 Conclusion

We asked whether a short tactile probe can make a certified minimal-force grasp gentler in its hold, and which component is responsible. In MuJoCo simulation, a ramp-down friction-onset probe combined with conformal risk control on the physical binary slip loss reduces mean relative excess hold force by D = 0.4168 (97.5 % CI [0.3484, 0.4778]) relative to the strongest non-tactile conformal baseline, selected beforehand, at a test physical slip rate of 0.0829 for α = 0.10, under a bound that holds in expectation per disturbance stratum and that requires simulator replays of every calibration grasp. This figure is conditional on an assumed onset noise of σ = 0.2 and a friction range of [0.3, 1.0]: it falls to 0.1401 at σ = 0.4, and in a narrow-friction, mid-amplitude sub-pool the method is worse than the baseline (D = −0.2614). The friction information comes from the probe: a one-line Coulomb plug-in on the onset is on its own superior to the baseline, and at the primary onset noise the learned model shows no demonstrated gain over that plug-in (not confirmed; no equivalence is claimed), so the method is the probe, a simple force rule and the certificate, with the learner optional. A non-tactile friction prior of log-noise 0.2 leaves no shown advantage. Under shift, frozen calibration breaks while binary-feedback quantile tracking keeps window slip near α, also with physical feedback in a descriptive study; whether it does so within 10 % of the force of oracle recalibration is inconclusive. Of four pre-registered core decisions, one was confirmed. Future work should measure onset noise on a physical gripper with a real tactile sensor, compare against reactive slip tightening and a friction-estimate controller with tuned per-stratum margins at matched slip, and run the online physical-feedback calibration on hardware, where binary slip is the only signal available.

## Reviewer A (robotics / tactile manipulation perspective)

**Scores:** Soundness 3/4 · Presentation 2/4 · Contribution 2/4 · **Overall 4/10 (borderline reject)** · Confidence 4/5

### Strengths
1. **Unusually honest, pre-registered evaluation.**
   - The confirmatory record is stated plainly: 1 of 4 core decisions confirmed.
   - The failed monotonicity gate is reported and drives the method change.
   - Deviations are listed (App. B), and every secondary number names its route and grid.
   - I found no number that the outputs do not support.
2. **The attribution is clean and useful.** The probe is designed so that the onset is the only friction channel:
   - fixed preload F₀ = 32.7 N;
   - static touch verified to carry no friction information (`19_report_rows.json` static_touch_check);
   - nested feature sets.

   The result that a one-line Coulomb plug-in on the onset captures essentially all of the saving is valuable and counter-hype (CSF-μ̂ vs CSF-1: D = 0.4149 on the refined grid, against 0.4289 for the learned arm). So is the finding that onset-only is never worse than the full pipeline (six route × grid rows).
3. **Conditions are put up front.** The σ curve (0.6789 → 0.1401), the narrow-friction reversal (−0.2614) and the CSF-proxy result at σ_p = 0.2 are in the abstract, not buried.

### Weaknesses
1. **The "minimal-force" framing is weak relative to the protocol.** I recomputed from the raw labels (`results/v1/labels/id_F*.jsonl`, 2,800 test instances):
   - F\*_mid has median 3.18 N, mean 4.90 N, 95th percentile 14.5 N and maximum 31.9 N.
   - Every object first receives a 32.7 N preload for 0.4 s, a 49 N squeeze peak, and a forced incipient slip.
   - So every grasp is pressed at roughly 10× its median minimal force before the hold. Since max F\* < F₀, simply holding at F₀ would never slip on this pool.
   - The saving the paper certifies is about 0.33 in relative excess hold force, on the order of 1–2 N absolute for a typical object.

   The paper acknowledges that peak force is not reduced (§1, §7). It does not quantify the hold-vs-protocol force ratio, the hold duration needed for the saving to dominate the integrated force, or whom the result serves. Fragile objects are excluded, and robust objects tolerate excess force.
2. **The comparator is weak by construction.** Friction is drawn log-uniformly over a 3.3× range and independently of every non-tactile input, so any friction-blind rule must pay for the whole range.
   - "Strongest non-tactile conformal baseline" means the best of three pre-chosen friction-blind rules.
   - The one informative stronger baseline, a friction prior at σ_p = 0.2, erases the advantage: D = −0.0299 [−0.1903, 0.0816].
   - The natural tactile competitors were not run: reactive slip-tightening, a friction-estimate controller with a tuned per-stratum margin, and a correlated-μ pool. The paper says so (§2, §7). As a result, the paper cannot claim practical relevance for tactile grip control.
3. **Simulation only, with an idealised sensor.**
   - The static touch record equals the commanded normal force and m g/2 exactly in the raw files (e.g. `touch_preload = [32.7, −0.8908, …]`).
   - Onset noise σ is assumed, and the headline is very sensitive to it.
   - The detector's intrinsic noise-free error (SD 0.11 log units, 0.20 for capsules) is already of the same order as the assumed σ.
   - No hardware anchoring of σ is attempted, not even from published onset-detection errors (which 09 §C planned to search for).
4. **The narrow-friction reversal undercuts generality and is under-analysed.** A back-of-envelope calculation explains it:
   - In the sub-pool μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8], the log-μ spread has SD ≈ 0.136.
   - The probe's effective log-noise is ≈ √(0.2² + 0.11²) ≈ 0.23, larger than the friction spread itself.
   - So the reversal is expected, not anomalous, and practitioners need this rule of thumb.

   The analysis also has gaps:
   - It is on the label route, whose premise failed.
   - Only about 88 calibration instances per stratum are available.
   - No arm-level RE or slip is reported.
   - It confounds narrow μ with mid amplitude.
5. **Presentation.**
   - The text is dense with route and grid qualifiers, hypothesis codes and repeated caveats.
   - The Discussion largely restates §5.
   - Fig. 1 has three panels with numerous annotations, which will not be readable at column width.

### Actionable revisions (Reviewer A)
- Report absolute forces:
  - the F\*_mid distribution against F₀ and 1.5 F₀;
  - absolute mean hold force per arm;
  - the integrated force over protocol + hold for representative hold durations.

  State the hold duration at which the hold saving dominates.
- Add the rule-of-thumb reading of the σ curve and the sub-pool reversal: effective onset noise versus friction log-spread.
- Cut the main text to 9 pages. Move the label-route rows, the σ-ordering paragraph and most of §5.5 to the appendix, and drop the restatement in §6.

## Reviewer A (robotics and tactile manipulation)

### Topic adherence
The paper stays on its declared topic: a ramp-down friction-onset probe, CRC on the physical binary slip loss, a comparison against the strongest non-tactile conformal baseline, an attribution ablation and an online binary-feedback study under shift. No environment or tooling issue is presented as a contribution. The appendix on compute and deviations is appropriate housekeeping. Two places drift toward over-elaboration rather than off-topic material: the label-route secondary rows, and the long hedging around every secondary number.

### Strengths
1. **A clear, honest experimental question with a fair comparator.** The comparator was selected on a disjoint split before calibration and test data were read (CSF-1 at 0.7686 against Mondrian-CSF 0.8294 and CertGrip-P 0.8335; verified in `comp_selection.json`). The friction-free preload, identical for every arm, is a clean design for isolating the probe as the only friction channel.
2. **The attribution story is unusually candid, and the data support it.** The simple Coulomb plug-in on the onset is about as good as the learned model. Onset-only beats CertGrip-full on all six route × grid rows (verified: 0.4464/0.4168, 0.4412/0.4289, 0.4736/0.4563, 0.4629/0.4475, 0.4148/0.4043, 0.4303/0.4015). Static touch and the squeeze add nothing. The paper correctly concludes that the method is "probe + simple rule + CRC".
3. **Failure regimes are reported.** These are the σ curve (D 0.6789, 0.5825, 0.4168, 0.1401), the narrow-friction sub-pool reversal (D −0.2614) and the S2 oracle that itself slips above α. The scope exclusions (peak force, fragile objects, micro-slip during the probe) are stated plainly.
4. **The results reproduce exactly.** I recomputed the headline D = 0.4168, all per-stratum slips and the λ̂ indices from the raw replay files without using the repository's analysis code.

### Weaknesses
1. **Simulation only, with a modelled sensor and an assumed noise level.** That is the central problem for an RSS tactile paper. The whole effect size is set by σ, which is assumed, not measured. D drops from 0.68 to 0.14 between σ = 0 and σ = 0.4, so without even a bench measurement of onset-force repeatability on a real sensor the headline cannot be placed on the curve. The contact model is rigid MuJoCo with pads sharing the object's μ, and disturbances are body forces with the hand still. RSS reviewers will expect at least one hardware experiment, or a measured σ, for a tactile grip-regulation contribution.
2. **The "minimal force" framing conflicts with the protocol.** Every object, including 50 g objects whose F\* is below 1 N (for example F1 object 0: F\*_mid 0.81 N), first receives 32.7 N for 0.4 s and a 49 N squeeze. In the simulated episode (0.4 s preload, 0.3 s squeeze, 0.25 s probe, 0.6 s disturbance window), the force-time integral of the shared protocol is comparable to or larger than that of the hold for most objects. The 42 % saving therefore applies to a fraction of the force the object actually experiences. The paper acknowledges this, but RE is the only efficiency metric reported. Peak force, impulse and absolute (N) excess per arm are missing, and no adaptive-preload variant was run. That variant would show whether the probe still works when the preload is not 30 to 600 times the weight.
3. **No tactile competitor.** Related Work names two families (reactive slip tightening, and friction estimation plus a safety factor) as the natural competitors, and neither is evaluated. The only stand-in, B2 (probe plus one uniform margin), is weak by construction, as the paper admits: the D3/D4 plug-in ignores the hidden load. RAW-Qv shows that one validation-tuned shift already lands near α in distribution. A per-stratum validation-tuned margin on CSF-μ̂ would therefore very likely match CRC in force and roughly in slip. Without it, the practical added value of the certificate over standard practice is unquantified.
4. **Is this tactile at all?** The friction information is carried by the onset force of a ramp-down. Static touch is uninformative in this model, and the detector is a ratio threshold on summed shear and normal readings. Gripper-side signals (motor current and encoder), wrist F/T or vision of object motion could plausibly detect the same onset. The title's "tactile" claim needs either a non-tactile onset-detector ablation or a narrower wording.
5. **Robot-side risk of the probe.** The probe drives every object to incipient slip. The maximum displacement at onset is 1.92 mm against a 2 mm slip threshold (verified: median 0.126 mm, p95 0.254 mm). On real hardware, with sensor latency and less clean signals, some fraction of probes would become slips or drops. That rate is the dominant safety number for deployment and is not estimated, for example with added detector latency.
6. **The online result under shift is mixed for the probe arm and the text understates it.** Frozen-CertGrip breaks more than Frozen-COMP under S3 (window slip 0.2461 against 0.1910, `h3_analysis.json`), which suggests that the tactile arm is more fragile to amplitude shift. Table 3 shows this, but Section 5.6 does not mention it. The B3 shifted windows skip steps 501 to 1,000, where the catch-up transient occurs (audited physical slip 0.134 for OQT-CertGrip in S1, steps 501 to 1,000). The catch-up cost is therefore not reported for the physical-feedback route a robot would use.
7. **Length and readability.** The main text runs to about 10,300 words (Abstract 252, Introduction 1,197, Related Work 693, Method 2,242, Setup 1,155, Results 2,879, Discussion 1,019, Limitations 551, Conclusion 306), plus about 1,900 words of appendix. No section is under the minimum lengths in the task, but the paper is far over an RSS 8-page budget. Every number carries route, grid and adjustment qualifiers, and hypothesis codes still appear in table rows. A robotics reader will struggle to find the message.

### Actionable revisions (Reviewer A)
- Paper-level: rewrite the efficiency claim as "hold force after a fixed high-force protocol". Add peak force, impulse and absolute excess (N) per arm; these can be computed from existing replays and labels. Shorten the main text to fit 8 pages by moving label-route rows, H-codes and the second-round descriptive studies to the appendix. Mention Frozen-CertGrip's larger S3 break in the text. Report the 501 to 1,000 transient for the physical-feedback streams if those steps were logged.
- Evidence-level: run (i) a reactive slip-tightening controller and (ii) friction estimate plus a per-stratum validation-tuned margin, both compared at matched slip. Run an adaptive or low-preload variant. Ablate the onset detector with a non-tactile signal. Measure onset-force repeatability on at least one real sensor and gripper, or else retitle the paper as a simulation study of a probe primitive.

### Score (Reviewer A): **4 / 10 (borderline, leaning reject at RSS)**
The work is careful, reproducible and honest, but the contribution rests on an assumed sensor-noise level in rigid-contact simulation. It omits the tactile baselines it names, and its "minimal force" is measured after a protocol that applies far more force than the hold. With hardware grounding of σ and one tactile competitor it would move to a weak accept.

### Strengths
1. **The certificate is applied correctly.** The monotonised loss L̃ ≥ L, which is non-increasing in λ, the (nR̃ + 1)/(n + 1) ≤ α rule, per-stratum calibration and the treatment of unavoidable instances all match the CRC theorem (Angelopoulos et al.). Switching from split conformal on labels to CRC on the physical loss after the monotonicity gate failed, as the registered failure branch prescribed, is the right call.
2. **The inference is careful.** The bootstrap resamples objects (four instances per object move together) and refits λ̂ in every draw, so calibration uncertainty is included in the CI\*\*. Robustness to F\*_lo/hi, five predictor seeds, two grids, matched slip and LTT are all reported, and the paper correctly says that the route variants re-analyse the same replays and are not replications.
3. **The confirmatory ledger is transparent.** One of four core decisions was confirmed, and the deviations, including dropping Holm and the post hoc grid, are listed. The paper states the in-expectation, marginal and per-stratum nature of the bound and the 4δ union-bound caveat for LTT correctly.

### Weaknesses
1. **Misstated pre-registration of H2b** (see the summary). It must be corrected in five places. The current wording also weakens the paper's own argument: the correct account (registered TOST, run, failed on the decision route, passed on the label route) is more informative than "not registered".
2. **"σ and σ_p are different noise models and are never placed on one scale" is not defensible as written.** Because μ̂ = m g / (2 F_onset), log-noise σ on F_onset is log-noise σ on μ̂, on the same scale as σ_p, up to clipping and the detector's intrinsic error. From the label files (4,800 calibration and test instances) I computed the probe's effective error in log μ̂ − log μ. After removing the constant bias of about +0.32, which CRC absorbs, the SD is 0.098, 0.143, 0.227 and 0.417 at σ = 0, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.4. The correlation with log μ is 0.959, 0.916, 0.816 and 0.600. A CSF-proxy at σ_p = 0.2 has correlation about 0.87, so it is a **better** friction estimate than the probe at the primary σ = 0.2. The H5 null at σ_p = 0.2 is therefore exactly what one should expect, and it is the most interpretable result of H5. The paper should state this equivalence instead of disclaiming it, and report the probe's effective friction-estimation error at each σ.
3. **The H5 superiority rows at σ_p 0.4/0.8, and the "learner beats plug-in at σ = 0.4" finding, compare against dominated estimators.** CSF-proxy at σ_p 0.4 (RE 0.9213) and 0.8 (RE 2.786), and CSF-μ̂ at σ 0.4 (RE 0.9877), are all *worse* than the friction-blind CSF-1 (0.7832). They plug a noisy reading in without shrinkage, while the prior SD of log μ is only 0.347. A Bayes or linear-shrinkage plug-in can never be worse than CSF-1. In my computation, linear shrinkage at σ = 0.4 lowers the residual log-friction SD from 0.417 to 0.278. So "held as predicted" at σ_p ≥ 0.4 is uninformative, and the learned model's advantage at σ 0.4 is plausibly just shrinkage. The paper's own D-μ diagnostic hints at this ("as if the learner partly shrank"). A shrunk plug-in is the right physics baseline.
4. **The empirical validity of the certificate is shown on one calibration draw only.** CRC bounds the expectation over calibration draws. The evidence is a single split, on which CSF-1 exceeds α in D1 (0.1157) and D3 (0.1057). On the refined grid, CertGrip-full exceeds α in D1 (0.1043) and D2 (0.1014); the paper lists only the primary-grid per-stratum slips, all at or below α, which reads more favourably than the full picture. Because every instance has been replayed on the full grid, repeated random calibration/test re-splits of objects need no new simulation. They would show directly that mean per-stratum test slip is at most α and how wide the realised-slip distribution is, which is the quantity a practitioner cares about. The bootstrap with replacement is not a substitute, because duplicated calibration instances change the order statistics.
5. **The certificate is not deployable, so the title's "certified" is simulation-only.** Both offline certificates need replays of the same grasp at all 20 or 41 grid forces. The deployable route (OQT) has only the long-run accounting identity, which the paper correctly calls "expected behaviour rather than a finding". The one deployable-route study (B3) has 5 orderings, no intervals, no S2, and omits the transient window. The statistical contribution is therefore an application of published tools (CRC, LTT, OQT) with no new guarantee. The paper says this. For RSS that is acceptable only if the robotics side carries the novelty, and in simulation it does so only partly.
6. **Grid design and multiplicity.** The primary grid (pooled q̂_sel ± 0.4) had a floor defect for the plug-ins in D1/D2, so the decision-bearing H2b estimate is biased towards the learned arm, as the paper admits. The refined grid is post hoc. Holm was registered and dropped. All this is disclosed, but nothing has been done to remedy it. The Holm-adjusted versions of the secondary families can be computed from existing bootstrap output and should be reported. LTT at δ/4 per stratum (family-wise δ) also needs no new simulation and should be added.
7. **H2-R, the reversal regime, rests on the weakest route.** It uses the label route, whose premise failed. The sub-pool has 352 calibration instances in total, about 88 per stratum, which makes the split-conformal quantile noisy. Its upper limit (−0.0325) would probably not survive multiplicity correction. CRC replays already exist for every calibration and test instance, so the sub-pool can be redone on the CRC route, and for onset-only and CSF-μ̂, without new simulation.
8. **Minor points.** (i) The H2a decision rule applies the SESOI to the point estimate only, which is why σ = 0.4 is still labelled "superiority" while its lower limit (0.0832) is below the SESOI. This is handled through σ\*, but it should be stated in Table 1. (ii) The H2-H heterogeneity Q = 31.04 on 15 df (p ≈ 0.009) indicates real cell heterogeneity, driven by D3, and should be called that rather than only "pass". (iii) The matched-slip comparison is test-selected, as the paper says. A calibration-set-located matched level would make it a deployable comparison.

Replace the "never on one scale" sentence with the effective friction-error SD and correlation of the probe at each σ.
