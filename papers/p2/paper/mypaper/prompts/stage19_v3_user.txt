# Probe, Then Calibrate: Conformal Slip-Risk Control of Grip Force from a Tactile Friction-Onset Probe (a Simulation Study)

## Abstract

We choose a low grip *hold* force whose physical slip rate is at most α. Friction-blind conformal baselines can bound slip but must cover the whole friction range. After a fixed preload, a ramp-down probe lowers the grip until a touch-only detector reports incipient slip, giving a Coulomb friction estimate. Any force rule fed with it (a one-line plug-in or a learned quantile model) is calibrated per disturbance stratum by conformal risk control (CRC) on the binary physical-slip loss. This gives a certified-slip bound in simulation that is marginal per stratum, holds in expectation and, as implemented, needs simulator replays. In MuJoCo at α = 0.10, the pre-registered comparison against the strongest non-tactile conformal baseline, chosen beforehand, reduces relative excess hold force by D = 0.4168 (97.5 % CI [0.3484, 0.4778]) at test slip 0.0829. Mean hold force is 6.521 vs 7.606 N. The saving comes from the probe: the learned model shows no demonstrated gain over the plug-in at onset noise σ = 0.2, and at σ = 0.4 a shrunk plug-in beats it. The result assumes onset noise (D 0.1401 at σ = 0.4) and friction drawn on [0.3, 1.0] independently of other inputs. In a band μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8] the baseline is better (D −0.2614; −0.3152 on the refined CRC grid). Online, binary physical-feedback tracking returns to α after shift (late-window slip 0.1001–0.1018, 20 orderings). Its efficiency against oracle recalibration is inconclusive.

## 1 Introduction

Many manipulation tasks need the smallest grip force that still keeps the object in the hand through the motions and loads of the task. Excess holding force loads the object and the actuator for the whole hold, and slip prevention at limited force is a recognised concern for prosthetic hands \cite{berman2025autonomous}. Too little force drops the object. Humans resolve this trade-off by scaling grip force to the friction they feel after a brief contact \cite{johansson1984roles} and by re-regulating when friction changes suddenly \cite{willemet2024grip}. Robotic tactile grip regulation pursues the same goal. Controllers monitor incipient slip and tighten the grasp \cite{dong2019maintaining, veiga2020grip, berman2025autonomous}, estimate friction online and add a safety margin \cite{khamis2021real, niu2026synchronized}, or learn a map from tactile signals to a gentle grasp force \cite{boonstra2024estimate}. None of them gives a quantitative, distribution-free statement of the form "this force rule slips on at most a fraction α of grasps". Their margins are set by hand or estimated, and the resulting slip rate is whatever the experiment shows.

Conformal methods provide exactly this kind of statement. Split conformal prediction \cite{lei2017distribution} and conformal risk control \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal} turn any predictor into a rule whose expected loss on an exchangeable test point is bounded by a target level. They need only a held-out calibration set and make no distributional assumptions. In grasping, the predictor proposes a force, and calibration inflates it until the slip risk on calibration grasps is at most α. The guarantee itself is cheap, but its price in force depends on what the predictor knows. A rule that cannot see friction has to cover the whole friction range with one inflation factor. With μ spanning more than a factor of three, the calibrated force is then conservative for most objects. The relevant comparator for a tactile method is therefore not an uncalibrated controller but the *strongest non-tactile conformal baseline*: a rule that uses everything a robot knows without touch and is calibrated to the same α. We selected this comparator on a disjoint selection split before any calibration or test data were read. A friction-blind Coulomb rule, CSF-1 (selection-split mean relative excess 0.7686), beat a Mondrian variant given privileged object-family identity (0.8294) and a learned proprioceptive quantile model (0.8335).

The paper asks one question in three parts. First, can a short tactile probe make a certified physical slip rate of at most α cheaper in hold force? Here "certified" means in simulation, marginal per disturbance stratum and in expectation. Second, which component carries the saving: the probe itself, or a learned model on top of it? Third, does calibration survive distribution shift when the only feedback available on a robot is whether the object slipped?

Our answer combines one mechanism with one statistical tool. The mechanism is the probe. After a fixed preload F₀ = 32.7 N, which is identical for every object and therefore says nothing about mass or friction, the gripper lowers its force until a touch-only detector reports incipient slip. Under Coulomb friction the onset force gives μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset). The tool is CRC on the binary physical slip loss, applied per disturbance stratum. We use the physical loss rather than simulator labels because the pre-registered monotonicity premise of the label rule failed in 8 of 800 instances. The offline calibration needs, for every calibration grasp, replays at every grid force, which only a simulator provides. The deployable route is online tracking from one observed binary outcome per grasp, which we study under three shifts.

**Contributions.**
- **(C1) Probe protocol and calibrated plug-in.** We give a grasp protocol in which a friction-uninformative preload leaves a ramp-down onset probe as the only friction channel. Per-stratum CRC on the monotonised physical binary slip loss turns a one-line Coulomb plug-in on that onset, or any other probe-fed rule, into a rule with marginal expected slip ≤ α in simulation.
- **(C2) Pre-registered evaluation and force accounting.** Against the friction-blind conformal baseline, the probe arm reduces relative excess hold force by D = 0.4168 [0.3484, 0.4778] and lowers mean hold force by 1.085 N on the primary test split. We also count what the fixed protocol costs: the hold saving recovers the probe-specific force-time after a 13.22 s hold.
- **(C3) Attribution and limits.** Ablations and baselines locate the saving in the probe: plug-in, shrunk plug-in, onset-only learner, feature chain, a non-tactile friction prior, uniform and tuned margins, and reactive slip-tightening controllers. We check empirically how the certificate behaves across object re-splits, strata and friction levels, report a sub-pool in which the method loses, and run an online binary-feedback study under three shifts.

Of the four pre-registered core decisions, one is confirmed: the probe arm beats the baseline. The label-validity premise and the learned-versus-plug-in decision are not confirmed, and online efficiency is inconclusive (Section 7). Section 2 positions the work. Section 3 defines the problem and method, Section 4 the protocol and statistics, and Section 5 reports results. Sections 6–8 discuss them, state limitations and conclude.

## 2 Related Work

**Friction reading and tactile grip control.** Reading friction from incipient slip is an established idea. Rubber-based tactile sensors were used early on to detect incipient slip and control grip \cite{marconi1996incipient}. Surveys of tactile friction estimation and slip detection \cite{chen2018tactile, romeo2020methods} describe a mature literature in which incipient slip, the partial slip at the contact periphery that precedes gross sliding, is the key friction cue, and in which the frictional properties of the interface determine the minimum grip force. Human precision grip adapts force to surface friction \cite{johansson1984roles} and to sudden friction changes \cite{willemet2024grip}. On the robot side, slip detectors on multifingered hands \cite{james2020slip}, learned temporal detectors on barometric sensors \cite{grover2022detect} and reactive multifingered controllers that combine slip onset with internal-force optimisation \cite{ayral2026reactive} trigger corrective tightening. Closer to minimal-force grasping, \cite{dong2019maintaining} keep grasps within slipping bounds by monitoring incipient slip, \cite{khamis2021real} estimate friction in real time and add a margin to the target force, \cite{boonstra2024estimate} learn to estimate incipient slip for gentle grasping, and \cite{niu2026synchronized} couple online friction estimation with adaptive grasp control. Learned and reflex controllers extend this to dynamic interaction \cite{kuo2025tracing}, vision-tactile reflexes \cite{feng2026tactilereflex} and sim-to-real force policies \cite{lach2024zero}. Adaptive analytical controllers come with Lyapunov arguments \cite{almohammed2022switched}, independent finger controllers stabilise grasps \cite{veiga2020grip}, and vision-language models select pre-grasp forces from experience \cite{shang2026exp}. In all of these the margin is reactive, hand-set or estimated, and none states a distribution-free bound on the resulting slip rate. We evaluate two stand-ins for this family: a reactive slip-tightening controller and an estimate-plus-tuned-margin rule (Section 5.3).

**Robust grasping under uncertainty.** Model-based approaches bound the risk of losing a grasp through assumed uncertainty sets. Grasping-force optimisation has been examined under parameter uncertainty \cite{cloutier2018examining}. Control barrier functions enforce friction-cone constraints \cite{cortez2019control} and, combined with disturbance observers and tactile sensing, give adaptive safe grasping \cite{kim2024robust}. Friction-informed risk margins \cite{enwerem2026firmgrasp} and variational belief parameterisations \cite{enwerem2026variational} make grasp synthesis tolerant of multimodal uncertainty. These guarantees hold relative to a model and its uncertainty set. Ours holds relative to the data-generating distribution of grasps without a friction model, at the price of being marginal over grasps and of needing calibration data from that distribution.

**Conformal prediction and risk control.** Conformal prediction gives finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees under exchangeability \cite{vovk2005algorithmic, lei2017distribution, angelopoulos2023conformal}. Conformalized quantile regression adapts the guarantee to the input \cite{romano2019conformalized}, and training-conditional \cite{bian2023training} and class-conditional \cite{ding2023class} variants refine the marginal statement. Conformal risk control generalises coverage to bounded monotone losses \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal}, with extensions to non-monotone losses \cite{angelopoulos2026conformal} and non-exchangeable data \cite{farinhas2023non}. Learn-then-Test gives high-probability risk control by multiple testing \cite{angelopoulos2025learn}. Under shift, adaptive conformal inference \cite{gibbs2021adaptive, gibbs2022conformal}, conformal PID control and quantile tracking \cite{angelopoulos2023conformala}, decaying step sizes \cite{angelopoulos2024online} and semi-bandit feedback \cite{ge2024stochastic} keep long-run coverage near the target. In robotics, conformal tools certify decisions \cite{lekeufack2024conformal}, verification and control \cite{lindemann2025formal, lindemann2023conformal}, motion planning among dynamic agents \cite{dixit2022adaptive}, contact-aware state estimation \cite{marques2026particle}, LLM planners \cite{ren2023robots}, sensorimotor policies under shift \cite{huang2023conformal}, RL safety filters \cite{strawn2023conformal}, safe Bayesian optimisation \cite{zhang2024bayesian}, interactive imitation learning \cite{zhao2024conformalized} and perception-planning pipelines \cite{kumar2026learnable}. In these works the controlled quantity is a prediction set, a trajectory region or a decision loss. None of them calibrates a physical contact outcome as a binary loss over a family of grip forces.

**Novelty.** Reading friction from slip onset is not new, and CRC, Learn-then-Test and score-space quantile tracking are used here as published. The paper contributes three things. First, a protocol in which the onset is the *only* friction channel, so that its value can be isolated. Second, a per-stratum, marginal bound on the *physical* slip rate of the resulting hold force, valid in simulation. Third, an attribution study showing that the calibrated saving comes from the probe and not from the learned model.

## 3 Problem Formulation and Method

### 3.1 Problem

A grasp instance x comprises an object (family, geometry, mass m, friction coefficient μ, centre-of-mass offset), a grasp pose offset, and a disturbance of known type d ∈ {D1, D2, D3, D4} (lift acceleration, shake, lateral pull, payload added mid-hold) with a hidden amplitude. A parallel-jaw gripper commands a hold force F, and slip(F, x) ∈ {0, 1} is the physical outcome of a full episode. A slip is a tangential displacement of the object relative to the pad frame above 2 mm, a rotation about the grasp axis above 3°, or loss of pad contact. The minimal no-slip force F\*(x) is bracketed in simulation by log-space bisection on [F_min, F_max] = [0.1, 100] N, using one replay at F_max and 10 halvings (11 replays). This gives F\*_lo < F\*_hi and F\*_mid = √(F\*_lo F\*_hi). A force rule is scored by its mean relative excess and its physical slip rate,

$$\mathrm{RE}(F) = \mathbb{E}_x\big[(F(x) - F^*_{\mathrm{mid}}(x))/F^*_{\mathrm{mid}}(x)\big], \qquad \mathrm{SR}(F) = \mathbb{E}_x[\mathrm{slip}(F(x), x)].$$

RE is signed, includes slipped instances, and is computed over avoidable instances (F\*_hi ≤ F_max). The task is

$$\min_F \ \mathrm{RE}(F) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathrm{SR}(F) \le \alpha,$$

with the constraint enforced without a contact model, from calibration instances drawn from the test distribution. Two rules A and B are compared on identical test instances by $D = 1 - \mathrm{RE}(A)/\mathrm{RE}(B)$, so D > 0 means that A needs less excess force. D is a reduction in relative excess, not a share of hold force. The F\* labels exist only in simulation.

### 3.2 Grasp protocol and the friction-onset probe

Every method runs the same protocol, so every method sees the same physical history up to the moment it chooses F. The gripper closes to a constant preload F₀ = 32.7 N (m g/μ with m_max = 1 kg and μ_min = 0.3) and holds it for 0.4 s. Because F₀ is the same for every instance, it encodes neither mass nor friction. A squeeze F₀ → 1.5 F₀ → F₀ follows over 0.3 s. Then comes the **ramp-down probe**: the force falls log-linearly from F₀ towards 0.005 F₀ over 0.25 s. A detector that reads only the pad touch grids computes the shear/normal ratio r = Σ_pads |shear| / Σ normal from noisy taxels and declares onset when the smoothed log r stops rising (constants in Table A1). At onset the force returns to F₀ within 0.02 s and holds for 0.1 s. The gripper then ramps to the chosen hold force F, settles, and runs the 0.6 s disturbance window. The slip reference is the pose before the probe, so a slip during the probe counts as a slip of the grasp.

The onset gives the Coulomb reading μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset), clipped to [0.1, 3]. Simulated touch is cleaner than a real sensor, so we add onset noise in log units, $\tilde f = \log F_{\mathrm{onset}} + \sigma\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ drawn once per instance and σ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4}. All core decisions use **σ = 0.2**, an assumed level. The same noisy onset is passed to every arm that uses it.

The protocol is fixed. Preload and squeeze are protocol constants that exceed every test instance's F\*_mid (Section 5.1), and the probe induces micro-slip on every grasp by design. The method chooses only the hold force, and "minimal force" in this paper refers only to that force.

### 3.3 Force rules

Every rule has the form

$$F(x;\lambda) = \min\big(F_{\max},\, F_{\mathrm{base}}(x)\, e^{\lambda}\big),$$

where λ is a log-force offset set by calibration. The rules differ only in $F_{\mathrm{base}}$ and in what it may read. Mass is given to every method, on the stated assumption that a robot can weigh the object during the lift. External disturbance amplitudes (D3, D4) are hidden from all methods.

- **Probe plug-in** (the simplest instance of the method): $F_{\mathrm{base}} = m(g + a_{\mathrm{cmd}})/(2\hat\mu)$, with μ̂ from the noisy onset.
- **Shrunk probe plug-in:** the same formula with $\log\hat\mu_s = a + b\log\hat\mu$. The coefficients (a, b) come from an ordinary-least-squares fit of log μ on log μ̂ over the selection split only (800 instances). At σ = 0.2 this gives a = −0.3858, b = 0.7397; at σ = 0.4, a = −0.4738, b = 0.4044.
- **Learned probe model (optional):** a gradient-boosted quantile predictor ĝ(x) at level τ = 1 − α. Its feature sets are nested: P (proprioception: disturbance type, commanded acceleration, log mass, aperture at contact) ⊂ S (+ static touch) ⊂ full (+ squeeze features) ⊂ learned probe model (+ onset). The onset-only model is P plus the onset feature.
- **Non-tactile baselines:** CSF-1, $F_{\mathrm{base}} = m(g + a_{\mathrm{cmd}})/(2\bar\mu)$ for D1/D2 and $m g/(2\bar\mu)$ for D3/D4, with $\bar\mu$ the geometric mean of the friction range. Mondrian-CSF uses the same formula with one offset per family × disturbance cell \cite{ding2023class}, so it is given privileged family identity. A non-tactile friction prior replaces $\bar\mu$ by $\tilde\mu = \mathrm{clip}(\mu\, e^{\sigma_p \varepsilon_p}, 0.1, 3.0)$, σ_p ∈ {0.2, 0.4, 0.8}. This prior is an oracle-side construction that asks how good a friction prior from material or vision would have to be; it is not a deployable estimator. The friction-blind conformal baseline was chosen from CSF-1, Mondrian-CSF and the learned proprioceptive model on the selection split, before any calibration or test access.
- **Uncalibrated and tuned tactile alternatives.** (i) *Probe + one uniform margin:* F = (1 + κ)·F_base^μ̂ with κ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0}. (ii) *Probe + empirically tuned per-stratum margin:* the smallest grid λ whose calibration physical slip is ≤ α, with no (n + 1) correction. (iii) *Reactive slip-tightening:* the hold starts at c times a nominal force, c ∈ {0.5, 0.75}. During the hold, the same noisy-taxel ratio is read every 10 ms and smoothed over 6 frames. On each detection of r̄ > r_safe on 2 consecutive frames, F ← min(F_max, 1.25 F), ramped over 20 ms, with at most 4 steps. The friction-blind variant (REACT-blind) uses r_safe = 0.8·μ_min and the CSF-1 nominal force. The probe-informed variant (REACT-probe) uses r_safe = 0.8·μ̂ and the plug-in force. A slip before tightening counts.

The noise models of the probe and the prior differ. Since μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset), noise on the onset force is noise on the friction estimate: σ perturbs log F_onset, and hence log μ̂, on top of the detector's own bias and error. σ_p perturbs a clipped log-normal friction prior. Equal nominal values of σ and σ_p are therefore not claimed to mean equal estimate quality.

### 3.4 Calibration on the physical binary loss

For an arm with base force $F_{\mathrm{base}}$ and calibration instance i, define the physical loss and its monotonised version on a grid Λ,

$$L_i(\lambda) = \mathbb{1}\{\text{slip in a direct replay at } F(x_i;\lambda)\}, \qquad \tilde L_i(\lambda) = \max_{\lambda' \ge \lambda,\ \lambda' \in \Lambda} L_i(\lambda').$$

$\tilde L_i$ is non-increasing in λ and $\tilde L_i \ge L_i$, so a risk bound on $\tilde L$ implies the same bound on physical slip.

**CRC (primary).** Within each stratum, with n = 500 calibration instances and $\tilde R_n(\lambda) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \tilde L_i(\lambda)$,

$$\hat\lambda = \min\Big\{\lambda \in \Lambda : \frac{n \tilde R_n(\lambda) + 1}{n + 1} \le \alpha\Big\},$$

and λ̂ = max Λ if no grid point qualifies. By \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal}, a test instance exchangeable with the stratum's calibration instances satisfies E[slip at F(x; λ̂)] ≤ α. The bound is marginal over instances and friction values, holds per stratum, and holds in expectation over the calibration draw. It is not a guarantee for one calibration set, one object or one friction level.

**Learn-then-Test (secondary).** Per stratum, λ is scanned from the top of the grid downwards and kept while the one-sided Clopper–Pearson upper bound at δ = 0.1 on the calibration physical risk is at most α \cite{angelopoulos2025learn}. This gives P(risk ≤ α) ≥ 1 − δ per stratum over the calibration draw, the training-conditional view of \cite{bian2023training}. It is not family-wise across strata.

**Grids.** The primary grid has 20 points, Λ = q̂_sel + linspace(−0.4, 0.4, 20), centred on the arm's label-rule conformal quantile on the selection split and pooled over strata. A post hoc refined grid with 41 points per stratum, q̂_sel,d + linspace(−0.8, 0.8, 41), serves as a sensitivity route. A grid-edge rule re-runs an arm on the refined-grid recipe if a stratum's λ̂ lands on index 0 or 19.

**Why the physical loss.** The pre-registered certificate was split conformal on bisection labels with the one-sided score $s = \log F^*_{\mathrm{hi}} - \log \hat g(x)$ \cite{romano2019conformalized}. It transfers to physical slip only if slip is monotone in F. The monotonicity gate tested this on 800 instances and failed: 8 instances were non-monotone, the one-sided Clopper–Pearson upper limit was 0.02115 > 0.02, and 7 of the 8 were under D2. CRC on the physical loss needs no monotonicity, so it became the primary route, and the label route is reported as secondary.

**Deployability.** The offline calibration needs a replay of each calibration grasp at every grid force, so it is a simulation procedure. The online tracker of Section 3.5 needs only one binary outcome per grasp.

```
Algorithm 1: probe, then calibrate (per arm, per disturbance stratum d)
input : base-force rule F_base (probe plug-in, shrunk plug-in or learned model), grid Lambda,
        calibration instances {x_i} in stratum d, level alpha
0  every instance: preload F0, squeeze, ramp-down probe -> F_onset (+ onset noise sigma); F_base(x) uses it
1  for i in calibration set, for lambda in Lambda:
2       L[i, lambda] <- slip of a direct replay at min(F_max, F_base(x_i) * exp(lambda))
3  Ltilde[i, lambda] <- max_{lambda' >= lambda} L[i, lambda']                       # monotonise
4  CRC: lambda_hat <- first lambda with (sum_i Ltilde[i, lambda] + 1)/(n + 1) <= alpha, else max(Lambda) (flag)
5  LTT: scan lambda downwards from max(Lambda); keep while CP_upper(risk at lambda; delta) <= alpha
6  deploy: preload, squeeze, probe -> F_onset; command F = min(F_max, F_base(x) * exp(lambda_hat))
```

**Complexity.** Calibration needs |Λ| direct replays per instance. Choosing λ̂ costs O(|Λ| n) per stratum, and Learn-then-Test adds one Clopper–Pearson bound per grid point. Because every calibration and test instance is replayed on the full grid, the bootstrap recomputes λ̂ in each draw without new simulation. At deployment the method costs one probe and a closed-form force.

### 3.5 Online calibration from binary feedback

On hardware, F\* labels and replays are unavailable, but whether the object slipped is observed after every grasp. Score-space online quantile tracking \cite{angelopoulos2023conformala} updates one log-force offset per stratum,

$$q_{t+1} = q_t + \eta\,(\mathrm{slip}_t - \alpha), \qquad F_t = \min\big(F_{\max},\, F_{\mathrm{base}}(x_t)\, e^{q_t}\big),$$

with η = 0.1 × IQR of the in-distribution calibration scores (η = 0.03412, frozen) and anti-windup that skips the update when F_t is capped at F_max. We track in score space rather than with the level update of adaptive conformal inference \cite{gibbs2021adaptive}, because a friction drop can move scores beyond the support of the calibration scores. The reference arms are a decaying step size \cite{angelopoulos2024online}, frozen offline calibration, and an oracle that recalibrates on labelled instances from the shifted distribution (label route). We study two feedback variants. The pre-registered variant uses label-rule feedback with the label-route warm start. The secondary variant uses physical direct-replay feedback with the CRC λ̂ warm start, which is the route a robot would use. With this update the long-run average slip over uncapped steps approaches α whatever the shift. The experiments therefore measure the transient, the force the tracker needs, and the comparison with frozen and oracle calibration, not whether the tracker reaches α in the long run.

## 4 Experimental Setup

**Simulator and objects.** All experiments run in MuJoCo 4.0.0 (timestep 0.002 s, elliptic friction cone, impratio 10, condim 6). The parallel-jaw gripper has a force-controlled closing axis and one touch grid per pad. Fast tactile simulators exist \cite{wang2022tacto, akinola2025tacsl, leins2025hydroelastictouch}, and sensor configuration matters for learned manipulation \cite{luu2025manifeel, birtalan2026impact}. We use MuJoCo's pad grids with the explicit noise model of Section 3.2. The in-distribution families are F1 boxes, F2 cylinders, F3 capsules and F4 off-CoM composites. F5 thin plates are held out for the new-family shift. Friction is log-uniform on [0.3, 1.0], drawn independently of family, geometry, mass, CoM offset and disturbance, so no non-tactile input carries friction information. Mass is log-uniform on [0.05, 1.0] kg. The strata are D1 lift acceleration U[1, 10] m/s², D2 shake (amplitude U[5, 20] mm at U[2, 5] Hz), D3 lateral pull U[0.5, 2]·m g and D4 payload U[0.2, 1.0]·m. A static-touch check is consistent with the preload carrying no friction information: the summed pad normal reading differs from F₀ by at most 0.1597 N, and no instance slipped during the preload.

**Splits.** Splits are by object within family: train objects 0–149, selection 150–199, calibration 200–324, test 325–499. Calibration has n = 500 instances per stratum (2,000 in total), and test has 2,800 (700 per stratum). The selection split is used only to choose the baseline, to centre the grids and to fit the shrinkage coefficients.

**Shifts and streams.** S1 lowers friction (μ × 0.6), S2 introduces family F5, and S3 scales every disturbance amplitude by 1.5. S1m (μ × 0.9) is reported but is not a primary shift. The pre-registered streams have T = 2,000 steps, with the shift from step 501, evaluation window 1,001–2,000, 20 orderings and label-rule feedback. The physical-feedback streams cover S1–S3 with 20 orderings each and report windows 1–500 (pre-shift), 501–1,000 (transient), 1,001–2,000 and 1,501–2,000, with 95 % intervals over orderings only.

**Statistics.** Efficiency is RE against F\*_mid with D as the effect size. Validity is the physical slip rate in direct replay. The four core decisions share a Bonferroni split of a 5 % family-wise level (one-sided 0.0125 each). CI\*\* denotes a two-sided 97.5 % percentile interval from 2,000 object-cluster bootstrap draws, which resample calibration and test objects within family and refit λ̂ in every draw. The main comparison requires D ≥ 0.10 (the smallest effect size of interest, SESOI), a CI\*\* lower limit above 0, and both again with F\*_lo and F\*_hi in place of F\*_mid. The learned-versus-plug-in contrast uses the same superiority rule plus a registered TOST equivalence test within ±0.10 (0.0125 per side). Matched-slip RE uses one target slip (0.10), found by interpolation between grid offsets inside every bootstrap draw. Because that slip level is read off the test set, matched-slip contrasts are descriptive. For the v1 secondaries, Holm's correction is applied within the registered secondary families. Second- and third-round rows are unadjusted and labelled; their decision labels are superiority (lower limit > 0), inferiority (upper limit < 0) or "not shown". The refined grid can mark a primary-grid result as unreliable but cannot upgrade it.

**Pre-registered decisions.** There are four: the probe arm against the baseline (H2a), decided as superiority; the learned model against the plug-in (H2b), not confirmed; label validity (H1), not confirmed; and online efficiency (H3), inconclusive.

**Deviations from the pre-registration.**
(1) The CRC route was written after the monotonicity counts were seen, but before any predictor existed.
(2) On the primary grid, the plug-in's λ̂ sat at index 0 in D1/D2 (a grid floor). This was handled by the post hoc refined grid.
(3) The pre-registration gives the selection objects once as 200–249 and elsewhere as 150–199; the code uses 150–199.
(4) The probe arm's shift prediction was never specified and is untested. A friction diagnostic was computed on calibration plus test objects. The noisy-mass draw was fixed in a later addendum. One equivalence test under S1 was evaluated after its read-out.
(5) The second- and third-round analyses were registered after the first- and second-round read-outs and reviews, and they cannot change a first-round decision.
(6) The reactive-controller detector was fixed after the third-round analyses of existing replays and the shrunk plug-in read-out, but before any reactive-controller code. A 3-object timing pilot printed 12 calibration-instance outcomes; no parameter was changed afterwards, and the optional CRC variant of the controller was dropped.
(7) The registered physical-replay online oracle was not produced.
(8) The tuned per-stratum margin is tuned on the calibration split, as registered.
Constants and learner settings are in Table A1, and compute is in Appendix B.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Main comparison and force in newtons

**At α = 0.10 the learned probe model has a lower relative excess hold force than the friction-blind conformal baseline (pre-registered, confirmed).** The learned probe model has RE 0.4567 [0.4079, 0.5033] against 0.7832 [0.6968, 0.8583] for CSF-1. This gives D = 0.4168 with CI\*\* [0.3484, 0.4778], decided as superiority. With F\*_lo and F\*_hi in place of F\*_mid, D is 0.4150 and 0.4186. **Route robustness and replication (secondary).** The saving holds at matched slip 0.10 (D 0.4563), under Learn-then-Test (0.4043), on the refined grid (0.4289), and with an empirically tuned per-stratum margin in place of CRC (0.4168); intervals are in Table 1. All of these are pool-level statements; Section 5.4 reports a sub-pool where the sign reverses.

**Force in newtons (secondary, descriptive, point values).** Over the 2,800 test instances, F\*_mid has 10 / 50 / 90 % quantiles of 0.9549 / 3.184 / 11.35 N and a maximum of 31.87 N. The share of instances above the 32.7 N preload is 0.0. Mean excess over F\*_mid is 1.623 N against 2.708 N. On this single test split, the learned model's mean hold force is 1.085 N below CSF-1, with no interval.

**What the protocol costs.** The phases shared by every arm (preload, squeeze and ramp-down) integrate to 27.4258 N·s. Of this, the probe-specific squeeze and ramp-down account for 14.3458 N·s. Integrated force at hold times T = 1 / 5 / 30 s is 33.95 / 60.03 / 223.1 N·s for the learned model and 35.03 / 65.46 / 255.6 N·s for CSF-1. Compared with a friction-blind grasp that skips the probe-specific phases, the break-even hold time is 13.22 s for the learned model, 13.19 s for the onset-only model and 26.35 s for the plug-in at σ 0.2. Counting all shared phases as probe cost (an upper bound), it is 25.28 s for the learned model. For holds shorter than about 13 s, the probe's own force-time outweighs the hold saving. ### 5.2 Where the saving comes from

**A one-line plug-in on the same onset already captures the saving. The learned model shows no demonstrated gain at σ = 0.2, and at σ = 0.4 a shrunk plug-in is better (secondary unless stated).**

*Probe-fed rules against CSF-1.* The probe plug-in reaches D = 0.4149 [0.3322, 0.4890] on the refined grid. Its primary-grid value of 0.3816 is biased by the grid floor. The shrunk plug-in reaches 0.435 on the primary grid (with λ̂ at index 0 in D1–D2), 0.4522 under the edge rule and 0.4484 with all arms on the same refined-recipe grid; at σ = 0.4 it reaches 0.2454. The onset-only model reaches 0.4464 [0.3776, 0.4906]. All of these are superiority. The probe, a simple force rule and CRC are therefore sufficient for a large saving over the friction-blind baseline.

*Learned model against plug-in at σ = 0.2.* The registered contrast is D = 0.0569 [−0.0399, 0.1454], not confirmed. The label-route estimate, 0.0134 [−0.0850, 0.0814], lies within ±0.10, but it is secondary and not decision-bearing. None of the 8 new direct contrasts (refined-grid CRC, Learn-then-Test and matched slip on both grids, and three rows against the shrunk plug-in; Table 2) is shown. Their points range from −0.06463 to 0.05379, and 6 of the 8 lie below the registered point estimate 0.05692. *Across σ.* At σ = 0.4 the shrunk plug-in beats the learned arm: D(learned vs shrunk) = −0.1395 [−0.1832, −0.01264], inferiority, the opposite of the registered prediction. ### 5.3 Tactile competitors and calibration alternatives

**Without calibration, a single margin cannot reach α in every stratum. A margin tuned per stratum on the calibration data lands where CRC does on this split. The registered reactive controllers miss α by a wide margin.**

*Probe + one uniform margin (second round, descriptive).* With κ ≤ 0.5 the rule under-grips (negative RE, slip ≥ 0.6182). At κ = 1.0 overall slip is 0.3350: D1 and D2 are over-protected, while D3 and D4 slip at 0.6571 / 0.6186 (Table 3). The plug-in force for D3/D4 cannot see the hidden external load, so no uniform margin fits both types of stratum.

*Probe + empirically tuned per-stratum margin (third round).* On the primary grid, this rule reproduces the CRC test points for the learned model, the plug-in and CSF-1; for the plug-in, slip is 0.06571 and RE 0.4843. The refined-grid plug-in moves to slip 0.07964 and RE 0.4409, against 0.075 / 0.4551 under CRC, so there the (n + 1) correction costs 0.0142 RE. The tuned plug-in's D against CSF-1 is 0.3816 [0.3014, 0.4517] on the primary grid and 0.4331 [0.3344, 0.4956] on the refined grid. At 500 calibration grasps per stratum, CRC adds a finite-sample guarantee rather than a different operating point.

*Reactive slip-tightening (third round, descriptive).* REACT-blind with c = 0.5 / 0.75 slips at 0.765 / 0.4632, with mean 2.501 / 3.099 tightening steps. REACT-probe slips at 0.9921 / 0.9514 and takes about half a step per instance (Table 3). Every arm and stratum slips at ≥ 0.3086. This registered design fails under a criterion where slip before tightening counts. Detection timing is not reported, so we claim no reason for the failure and make no claim against reactive control in general.

### 5.4 How the certificate behaves, and where the method loses

**Across 200 object re-splits, the mean test slip stays below α for every rule, arm and grid. The bound is marginal, though: single strata and low-friction objects exceed α often.** These rows check the certified bound (simulation, marginal per stratum, in expectation) empirically.

*Re-splits (descriptive).* The 200 re-splits re-partition the same 4,800 instances, so they measure split-to-split variability, not performance on new objects. Under CRC, mean slip is 0.07121–0.08646, and the share of splits with overall slip above α is between 0.0 and 0.12. Under Learn-then-Test, mean slip is 0.06199–0.07102. For the learned model, at least one stratum exceeds α in 0.57 (primary) / 0.69 (refined) of splits; for CSF-1 the shares are 0.41 / 0.615. The largest single-stratum share is 0.445 (learned model, D2, refined grid).

*Single-split tables.* On the primary grid, every stratum of the learned model is at or below α (0.0871 / 0.0714 / 0.0757 / 0.0971), while CSF-1 exceeds α in D1 and D3 (0.1157 / 0.1057). On the refined grid the learned model exceeds α in D1 and D2 (0.1043 / 0.1014), and CSF-1 in D3 and D4 (0.11 / 0.1057). The seed-4 refit has overall slip 0.1018. By friction quintile from lowest to highest μ, slip runs from 0.2161 to 0.003571 for the learned model and from 0.4804 to 0.0 for CSF-1. The plug-in is flatter (0.08571 to 0.02857). The largest family × stratum cell for the learned model (n 175) is F1D1 at 0.1257.

*Validity of labels.* The label monotonicity gate failed (8 / 800; the largest slip force above F\*_hi exceeds it by a relative 0.00904). The check of labels against physical slip passes for all four arm × α combinations (physical minus label Δ between −0.00429 and −0.00143).

*Where the method loses.* On the label route, in the sub-pool μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8] with mid-range disturbance amplitude (352 calibration / 548 test instances), D = −0.2614 [−0.5320, −0.0325]. Its Holm-adjusted p is 0.03 within its family and 0.12 over all 52 first-round secondaries. On the CRC route the picture depends on the grid. On the refined grid D is −0.3152 [−0.5231, −0.0172] (inferiority); with the μ restriction alone it is −0.1717 [−0.4275, −0.01539], whose interval contains −0.3152; with the amplitude restriction alone it is +0.4929 (superiority). On the primary grid the same three sub-pools give +0.3754 / +0.3387 / +0.5004 (superiority). Within the sub-pool, CSF-1 runs at slip 0.03285 (RE 0.566) on the primary grid and at 0.1004 (RE 0.2633) on the refined grid, where the learned model slips at 0.08394. The reversal thus appears with the μ restriction and not with the amplitude restriction. It also comes with a higher CSF-1 slip, so slip is not matched, and it depends on the grid. No mechanism is established. The plug-in shows the same pattern (refined −0.6804; μ only −0.3705).

### 5.5 Online calibration under shift

**Frozen calibration breaks under friction and amplitude shift. Binary-feedback tracking returns to α after a transient, and the probe arm keeps its force advantage. Efficiency against oracle recalibration stays inconclusive.** Unlike the offline bound, which is certified only in simulation and only marginally per stratum, the tracker needs one binary outcome per grasp.

## 6 Discussion

**What the evidence licenses.** In simulation, the friction information comes from the probe, and per-stratum CRC makes the resulting saving certifiable in a marginal, per-stratum, in-expectation sense. A one-line plug-in on the onset, shrunk or not, captures the saving, and the learned model shows no demonstrated gain over it. The operative method is therefore *probe + simple force rule + CRC*, with the learner as an option whose value is not established. The saving amounts to about 1.085 N of mean hold force at α = 0.10, and it pays back the probe's own force-time only for holds longer than about 13 s. A per-stratum margin tuned on calibration data lands at the same operating point on this split. What CRC adds is the finite-sample guarantee, not a different force.

**The headline is conditional.** Two ingredients set the size of D: how much friction varies across the objects a robot meets, and how precisely its sensor reads the onset. In our pool, friction varies over [0.3, 1.0] independently of every non-tactile input, which forces any friction-blind rule to pay for the whole range. The onset noise is assumed. When the friction range is narrowed to [0.5, 0.8], the advantage disappears on the label route and on the refined CRC grid. When the onset noise doubles to σ = 0.4, D falls to 0.1401. The figure is one point on a curve, not a property of tactile grip regulation.

**Why the learner does not help here.** At σ = 0.2 the learned model and the plug-in read the same noisy onset. None of the eight direct contrasts separates them, and the registered equivalence test is not met either, so we claim neither a gain nor equivalence. At σ = 0.4 the earlier advantage of the learner over the raw plug-in disappears once the plug-in is shrunk on the selection split, and the shrunk plug-in becomes the better rule. On these features, the data show no gain from learning over a calibrated Coulomb formula on the onset. A learned component may still pay off with richer tactile signals, but that is not tested here.

**Relation to prior work.** Friction-estimating controllers \cite{khamis2021real, niu2026synchronized} and incipient-slip methods \cite{dong2019maintaining, boonstra2024estimate} leave the safety margin as a design parameter. Our results suggest that the same cue plus a model-free calibration step yields a large saving over friction-blind rules, and that learned tactile components should be compared against a calibrated plug-in on the same cue. Whether probe plus CRC beats such controllers at equal slip is not shown: our reactive stand-in is one registered design, and the tuned margin is our own construction. The online results agree with quantile-tracking theory \cite{angelopoulos2023conformala}. The tracker needs only the binary outcome that a robot observes, which makes it the natural route to hardware.

**Practical implications.** The probe adds well under a second per grasp, but it drives every object to incipient slip on every grasp. In simulation it found an onset on every in-distribution instance and displaced the object by a median 0.126 mm at onset (95th percentile 0.2543 mm, maximum 1.92 mm). Objects for which sub-millimetre slip or the 1.5 F₀ squeeze is unacceptable are outside its scope.

## 7 Limitations

Each caveat is stated here once in full and referenced elsewhere.

1. *Simulation only; σ assumed.* The study uses one simulator with rigid contact, five object families and a modelled detector. σ = 0.2 sets the effect size: D runs from 0.6789 to 0.1401 as σ goes from 0 to 0.4. We did not ground σ on hardware or in published detector error, so where a real sensor sits on the curve is unknown.
2. *Deployability.* Offline CRC needs replays at every grid force. The deployable route is online physical-feedback tracking. It was studied in simulation on 3 shifts × 20 orderings with intervals over orderings only, and it shows transient overshoot to 0.1389 / 0.1368 in S1 / S3. No physical-replay oracle exists.
3. *Marginal guarantee.* The bound holds per stratum and in expectation. For the learned model, single strata exceed α in a majority of re-splits (0.57 / 0.69), and the lowest-friction quintile slips at 0.2161. Learn-then-Test holds per stratum at δ = 0.1, not family-wise.
4. *Validity premise.* The label monotonicity premise failed, and the CRC route and the refined grid were adopted after that failure.
5. *Fixed protocol.* The 32.7 N preload and the squeeze exceed every test instance's F\*_mid, and the probe induces micro-slip on every grasp. The minimal-force claim applies to the hold force only, and no adaptive or low preload was run.
6. *Friction distribution and competitors.* μ is drawn independently of all non-tactile inputs. We ran no pool with correlated friction and no deployable vision or category prior; the non-tactile friction prior is oracle-side. We evaluated one registered reactive design, without detection-timing data.
7. *Where it loses.* For μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8], the friction-blind baseline is better on the label route and on the refined CRC grid, but not on the primary grid. Slip is unmatched there, and the cause is untested.
8. *Learned model.* There is no matched-slip shrunk-plug-in row, and the σ 0 / 0.1 learned-versus-plug-in rows have no refined-grid contrast.
9. *Multiplicity.* Holm covers only the first-round secondaries (52 rows, 40 rejected; no first-round verdict changes). Second- and third-round rows are unadjusted. Three third-round inferiority rows have upper bounds within 0.02 of 0 (−0.0172, −0.01539, −0.01264). A bootstrap p of 0.0 means p < 0.001, not 0.

## 8 Conclusion

We asked whether a short tactile probe can make a slip-bounded grasp cheaper in hold force, and which component carries the saving. In MuJoCo simulation, a ramp-down friction-onset probe combined with per-stratum conformal risk control on the physical binary slip loss reduces relative excess hold force by D = 0.4168 (97.5 % CI [0.3484, 0.4778]) against the strongest non-tactile conformal baseline, selected beforehand. The bound is marginal per stratum and in expectation. On the primary test split, mean hold force is 1.085 N lower, and the probe's own force-time is recovered after a 13.22 s hold. The method is the probe, a one-line (optionally shrunk) Coulomb plug-in and CRC. A learned model is optional and shows no demonstrated gain. The result is conditional on an assumed onset noise and a wide friction range that no non-tactile input predicts, and in a mid-friction band μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8] the baseline is better on the label route (D −0.2614). Under shift, binary-feedback tracking is consistent with convergence to α, while its efficiency against oracle recalibration is inconclusive. Three next steps follow. First, measure onset noise on a physical gripper with a real tactile sensor. Second, add an adaptive preload so that the protocol's force-time no longer dominates. Third, run online physical-feedback calibration on hardware, where binary slip is the only signal available.

### 2.1 Force-time accounting leaves out probe-specific force-time (substantive, fix required)
`analysis_v3_a.py::a7_force` counts the shared phases as preload (F₀ × 0.4 s), squeeze (mean recorded normal force × 0.3 s) and ramp-down (analytic log-linear integral up to onset). In `sim.py::_rd_touch_probe`, the probe then **returns to F₀ over t_up = 0.02 s and holds F₀ for 0.1 s** (`self._step(int(0.1 / DT))`) before the ramp to the hold force. The qualitative conclusion ("short holds do not pay back the probe") gets stronger, not weaker.

## Reviewer A (conformal prediction / statistical ML perspective)

**Summary.** The paper calibrates the hold force of a parallel-jaw grasp with split CRC on a monotonised binary physical-slip loss, per disturbance stratum. **Strengths.**
1. Negative and inconvenient results are reported: H1 failed, H2b was not confirmed, H3 was inconclusive, the mid-friction sub-pool reverses, and the shrunk plug-in beats the learner at σ 0.4.

## Reviewer B (robotic grasping / tactile manipulation perspective)

**Summary.** A simulation study of minimal-hold-force grasping. A fixed 32.7 N preload and a squeeze are followed by a ramp-down probe that stops at a touch-detected incipient slip, giving μ̂. A Coulomb plug-in or a learned quantile model sets the hold force, which is inflated per disturbance type by conformal risk control so that the physical slip rate is at most α in expectation.

**Strengths.**
1. A clean experimental design for *isolating* the value of the friction cue. The friction-uninformative preload and the iid friction draw make the onset the only friction channel, and the feature chain confirms that static touch and the squeeze add nothing.
2. The paper reports absolute newtons and force-time, not just relative excess, and admits that the fixed 32.7 N preload dominates the force budget, so the "minimal force" claim applies to the hold force only.
3. Credible tactile competitors are attempted: a uniform margin, a tuned per-stratum margin and two reactive slip-tightening controllers. Their failures are reported without overclaiming ("we make no claim against reactive control in general").
4. Honest practical caveats: the probe slips every object by a median 0.126 mm, and the protocol excludes fragile or slip-intolerant objects.

**Weaknesses.**
1. **Simulation only, with an idealised touch model.** The recorded preload normal reading equals the commanded 32.7 N to within 0.16 N. MuJoCo rigid contact with an elliptic cone and condim 6 is far from real pad mechanics, which have viscoelasticity, a contact area that grows with force, and rate-dependent friction. The detector's noise-free friction reading already has a large bias (0.3186 in log units) and varies across families (SD 0.045–0.196). Real sensor behaviour is replaced by the assumed σ.
2. **The protocol dominates the energy budget.** The method saves about 1.085 N of hold force but spends a 1.5 F₀ squeeze and a probe. With the omitted return-and-hold segment included (§2.1), the break-even hold time is about 16.6 s against the probe phases and about 28.6 s against all shared phases. For many pick-and-place holds this means the protocol costs more than it saves. The paper should state this more prominently and, ideally, run the adaptive or low-preload variant it proposes as future work.
3. **The reactive baselines are not competitive.** REACT-probe slips on 95–99 % of grasps, which points to a detector or design failure, not a fair representative of reactive grip control. Without detection-timing data, and without a well-tuned reactive controller (for example a slip-margin controller in the style of Khamis et al. or Dong et al.), the claim that calibration is needed over classical tactile control is unsupported. The paper wisely does not make that claim, but the comparison then adds little.
4. **The friction distribution decides the outcome.** In a realistic setting, a vision or material category prior would correlate with μ. The paper's own oracle prior at σ_p = 0.2 already erases the advantage (D −0.0299). The mid-friction band reverses the sign on the label route and the refined CRC grid. The practical value therefore hinges on an untested correlation structure.
5. **The calibration needs simulator replays at every grid force,** so the offline certificate cannot be produced on hardware. The online tracker is the only deployable route, and it carries only long-run guarantees.
6. Minor: the Fig. 1a prompt calls the squeeze a "triangular bump", but the code uses a half-sine. The slip criterion (2 mm / 3°) is reasonable but should be justified against task needs. D2 hosts 7 of the 8 non-monotone instances, which deserves a sentence on the mechanism (stick-slip under shake?).

**Actionable revisions.**
- Correct the force-time accounting (§2.1). Promote the break-even analysis to the main contribution statement, and add a run with an adaptive or lower preload (for example a preload set from the weighed mass and μ_min), which the protocol allows.
- Replace or supplement REACT with a tuned reactive controller (grid-search r_safe and the step size on the calibration split, as was done for the tuned margin) and report detection latency.
- Add a pool with correlated friction (for example family-dependent μ ranges) and a deployable category prior. This is the most important missing competitor.
- Ground σ with a measured onset-force scatter on any real sensor, or at least from published detector repeatability.
- Report the mass-noise result and the D-FID result (§2.5), and flag the shrinkage OLS as needing ground-truth μ (§2.6).
- Render Figures 1–2, delete the "master draft only" appendix label, and cut §5 to fit 9 pages.

**Score: 4 / 10 (borderline, leaning reject for NeurIPS/ICML).** Careful and transparent, but simulation-only with an assumed noise level. The protocol costs more force-time than it saves on short holds, and the competitive baselines a robotics audience expects (tuned reactive control, correlated priors) are missing or weak.

## Reviewer A: robotics / tactile manipulation

### Summary
The paper proposes a fixed grasp protocol: a 32.7 N preload, a squeeze, then a log-linear ramp-down that stops at touch-detected incipient slip. The onset force gives μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset). A hold-force rule fed with μ̂ is calibrated per disturbance stratum by CRC on the binary physical-slip loss. In MuJoCo at α = 0.10, it uses 41.7 % less relative excess force than a friction-blind conformal Coulomb rule. Ablations show that a one-line plug-in on the onset captures most of the saving. A binary-feedback online tracker returns to α after three shifts.

### Strengths
1. **Honest framing.** The paper states plainly that the result is simulation-only, that the bound is marginal per stratum and in expectation, that offline calibration needs simulator replays, and that three of four pre-registered decisions are not confirmed or inconclusive. RSS reviewers rarely see this degree of candour.
2. **The right comparator.** Comparing against a *calibrated* friction-blind rule chosen beforehand on a disjoint split, rather than against an uncalibrated controller, is the correct design for asking what touch buys under a slip guarantee.
3. **The attribution result is useful to the community.** The learned model shows no demonstrated gain over a Coulomb plug-in on the same onset, and a shrunk plug-in beats it at σ = 0.4. That is a sobering, practically relevant message for learned tactile grip control.
4. **Force in newtons and force-time.** Reporting absolute hold forces, the preload's dominance and a break-even hold time is the kind of accounting robotics papers usually skip.
5. **Fully reproducible numbers.** Every headline and table value we recomputed from raw replays matched (Section 0).

### Weaknesses
**A-W1. The headline effect is close to true by construction (major).** Friction is drawn log-uniform on [0.3, 1.0] *independently of every non-tactile input*. So no non-tactile rule can carry friction information, and CSF-1 is nearly the Bayes-optimal friction-blind rule. D = 0.42 therefore mainly measures how wide the friction prior is relative to the probe's noise; it does not measure a property of tactile grip regulation. The paper says so in Section 6, which we appreciate. But the abstract and contributions still present D = 0.4168 as the result. The two oracle-side checks (friction prior σ_p = 0.2 gives D ≈ −0.03; the sub-pool μ ∈ [0.5, 0.8] reverses the sign) show that the effect vanishes when friction is either predictable or narrow. A realistic object set, with friction correlated with family, material or appearance, is needed before the 0.42 means anything for a robot.

**A-W2. The "where it loses" analysis is framed too generously to the method (major; must fix).** We recomputed the sub-pools.
- **The primary-grid superiority in the sub-pool is a grid-floor artefact.** Inside the μ ∧ amp sub-pool, CSF-1's λ̂ is at **index 0 in D1 and D2** (also for μ only). That is why CSF-1 runs at slip 0.0328, far below α, with RE 0.566. On the refined grid, CSF-1's λ̂ is at index 10 in every stratum, and its slip is 0.1004. The paper's own rule says "the refined grid can mark a primary-grid result as unreliable but cannot upgrade it", and the grid-edge rule (re-run if λ̂ is at index 0) should apply. Both point the same way: the primary-grid +0.3754 / +0.3387 should be marked unreliable, not presented as equal evidence ("the picture depends on the grid"; Limitation 7, "but not on the primary grid").
- **"Slip is not matched" does not explain the μ-only reversal.** On the refined grid with the μ restriction alone, CSF-1 slips *less* than the learned model (0.0821 vs 0.0858) and still needs less excess force (RE 0.3281 vs 0.3844; D = −0.1717). The unmatched-slip caveat applies only to the μ ∧ amp cell.
- **There is an obvious candidate mechanism.** The paper says none is established. But the log-width of [0.5, 0.8] is ln 1.6 ≈ 0.47, comparable to the onset's error in log units (σ = 0.2 added noise plus detector SD 0.11 and bias 0.32). A friction-blind rule wins when the friction spread is not much larger than the probe's error. This can be tested directly with the existing replays (D against the μ-band width at fixed σ) and would turn a "loss" into a usable decision rule for practitioners.

**A-W3. The probe's force-time is under-counted, so the break-even time is understated (major; must fix).** `code/analysis_v3_a.py::a7_force` integrates preload, squeeze and ramp-down only. `certgrip/sim.py::_rd_touch_probe` then returns to F₀ over 0.02 s **and holds F₀ for 0.1 s**. That phase exists only because of the probe; a no-probe grasp would go straight from preload to the hold ramp. On the test split it adds about **3.62 N·s** (mean F_onset is 2.20 N). With it included, the probe-specific cost is about 17.96 N·s, not 14.35 N·s, and the break-even rises from 13.22 s to **≈ 16.6 s** for the learned model. The all-phase bound rises from 25.28 s to ≈ 28.6 s, and the plug-in's from 26.35 s to ≈ 33.0 s. The figure appears in the abstract chain (C2), Fig. 1a, Table 1, Section 6 and the Conclusion. Correct it, or define the phases explicitly and justify the exclusion.

**A-W4. The reactive competitors are straw men (major for C3).** In `code/react_run.py`, both REACT arms start the hold at c ∈ {0.5, 0.75} × the **uncalibrated** nominal force (CSF-1's or the plug-in's `log_g`, with no λ̂ offset), and any > 2 mm slip before tightening counts. Starting at roughly half the estimated minimal force, with a detector that needs two 10 ms frames plus a 20 ms ramp, almost guarantees slip (REACT-probe 0.99 / 0.95). The paper hedges ("no claim against reactive control in general"). But C3 still lists "reactive slip-tightening controllers" among the ablations that "locate the saving in the probe". As run, they locate nothing. Either add a reactive variant that starts at the CRC-calibrated force and tightens on detection (the natural hybrid), or remove the reactive rows from the attribution claim and label them a negative control.

**A-W5. The "minimal force" is only the hold force; peak and preload forces are fixed and large.** Every object, including 50 g ones, sees 32.7 N for 0.4 s and 49 N at the squeeze peak, and is driven to incipient slip (median 0.13 mm, max 1.92 mm). For the motivating applications (fragile objects, prostheses, actuator load), peak force usually matters at least as much as hold force. The paper states this in Limitation 5. The Introduction's motivation ("excess holding force loads the object…") should be narrowed to match. An adaptive-preload experiment would make the paper much stronger for RSS.

**A-W6. Sensor realism.** σ = 0.2 is assumed and sets the effect size (D from 0.68 to 0.14 over σ = 0 to 0.4). No grounding from published incipient-slip detector error is attempted, although some of the cited works (Khamis 2021; Boonstra 2024; Dong 2019) report friction-estimate errors that could at least place σ on the curve. A literature-based bracket would cost one paragraph.

**A-W7. Mass is assumed known.** μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset) and every force rule use the true mass. The justification ("weigh during the lift") is not in the protocol, which runs the probe before the hold. There is a 10 % mass-noise secondary in `results/v1/sec08h_massnoise.json` (D 0.4603 [0.4106, 0.5050], label route), which supports robustness. It is mentioned only as a deviation. Report it in the main text.

**A-W8. Presentation.** The Results section mixes three amendment rounds, two grids, three calibration routes and label vs physical routes, with numbers to 4–5 significant figures. An RSS reader cannot easily tell what is established. Move most secondary rows to an appendix, and keep one table with H2a, the σ curve, plug-in vs learned and the sub-pool.

### Questions for the authors
1. Does the D-against-μ-band-width curve (from existing replays) show a crossover, and where is it relative to σ?
2. How do the CRC-calibrated hold force plus the reactive tightening hybrid perform?
3. Why is the 0.1 s re-hold at F₀ needed after onset? Could the probe return directly to the hold force (which would also shorten the break-even time)?

### Actionable revisions (Reviewer A)
1. Rewrite Section 5.4, Table 3 and Limitation 7: mark the primary-grid sub-pool superiority unreliable (CSF-1 at the grid floor); state that the μ-only reversal occurs at matched or lower CSF-1 slip; add the friction-spread vs probe-error analysis.
2. Recompute force-time with the post-onset return and 0.1 s hold, and update 13.22 / 25.28 / 26.35 s everywhere (abstract chain, C2, Fig. 1a, Tables 1, Sections 6 and 8).
3. Either add a calibrated-start reactive hybrid, or remove reactive controllers from the C3 attribution claim.
4. Add a correlated-friction pool (for example friction partly predictable from family) or a deployable category prior, so that "strongest non-tactile baseline" is not trivially friction-blind.
5. Move the mass-noise result into Section 5.1, and bracket σ with literature values.
6. Cut Results to the decision-bearing and mechanism-relevant rows; round to 2–3 significant figures in the main text.

**Score (Reviewer A): 4 / 10 (borderline reject).** Careful, honest and reproducible. But the main effect is largely set by a design choice (friction independent of all non-tactile inputs, with assumed σ), the force-time accounting has a real omission, the reactive comparison is uninformative, and there is no hardware. For RSS we would want either hardware onset-noise grounding or a non-trivial non-tactile baseline.

## Reviewer B: conformal prediction / statistical methodology

### Summary
The paper applies split CRC on a monotonised binary physical-slip loss, per disturbance stratum, over a log-force offset grid. The base force comes from a tactile friction estimate. It compares this, with pre-registered decisions, a Bonferroni split, object-cluster bootstrap CIs and many labelled secondaries, against a calibrated friction-blind rule. It also studies score-space online quantile tracking with binary feedback.

### Strengths
1. **Correct use of CRC.** Monotonising the loss on the grid (L̃ ≥ L, non-increasing) and evaluating physical L at λ̂ gives a valid bound E[L] ≤ E[L̃] ≤ α. The (n + 1) correction, the fallback to max Λ and the per-stratum exchangeability (each object appears once per stratum) are all correct. We checked the implementation independently.
2. **Statistical hygiene above the field norm.** There is pre-registration, a disjoint selection split for the comparator and grid centring, an object-cluster bootstrap that refits λ̂ in every draw, Bonferroni for the four core decisions, Holm for the first-round secondaries, explicit "unadjusted" labels, and a TOST that is run and reported as not met.
3. **Clear scope of the guarantee.** Marginal per stratum, in expectation over the calibration draw, not per object or per friction level. This is backed by re-splits (mean slip below α; single-stratum exceedance in 0.57 of splits), quintile tables and a Learn-then-Test high-probability variant. This is how conformal claims should be presented.
4. **Useful empirical finding on finite-sample cost.** At n = 500 per stratum, the empirically tuned margin lands on the same grid point as CRC on the primary grid. So the guarantee is nearly free on this grid. This is consistent with the (1 − α)/n ≈ 0.0018 correction being below the grid spacing.

### Weaknesses
**B-W1. The certificate is not obtainable on a robot as implemented (major).** Offline CRC needs, for each calibration grasp, the counterfactual slip outcome at **every** grid force (20 or 41 replays of the identical instance). On hardware you observe one outcome per grasp. The paper says so. But it then calls the deployable route (online tracking) the "natural route to hardware", and that route carries only a long-run-average guarantee with no per-stratum finite-sample statement. Its efficiency (H3) is inconclusive and its physical-replay oracle was not produced. So the paper's distinctive claim ("certified-slip") holds only where the simulator can replay. RSS reviewers will ask for a one-outcome-per-grasp calibration scheme. Possible routes: randomise λ per calibration grasp and use an inverse-propensity-weighted or monotone-interval CRC (one Bernoulli per grasp, monotonicity in F used to bound L̃); or a Learn-then-Test variant on randomised forces. At minimum, discuss this as the central open problem, not as a deployability footnote.

**B-W2. Data-dependent grid choices weaken the exactness of the guarantee (moderate).** The grid-edge rule re-runs an arm on a different grid *if λ̂ lands on index 0 or 19*. That decision is a function of the calibration data, so the reported λ̂ is the output of an adaptive procedure for which the CRC bound does not hold exactly. The refined grid was also adopted post hoc after the primary-grid floor was seen. The effect is probably small, but the text calls the edge-rule rows certified on the same footing. Either (i) state that edge-rule and refined-grid rows are not covered by the CRC theorem as run, or (ii) define the grid choice from the selection split only (for example, widen until the selection-split q̂ is interior), which keeps exchangeability.

**B-W3. The registered H2b contrast was biased against the plug-in, and the bias is only partly fixed (moderate).** On the primary grid the plug-in's λ̂ is at index 0 in D1/D2 (stratum slips 0.0586 / 0.0386 at σ = 0.2; 0.0071 / 0.0029 at σ = 0). The registered H2b point estimate 0.0569 is therefore inflated in favour of the learned model. The paper reports this and gives refined-grid rows. But the abstract's "no demonstrated gain" wording is, if anything, generous to the learner. The σ 0 / 0.1 refined-grid numbers (plug-in RE below the learned model's) suggest the plug-in may be *better* there. A refined-grid bootstrap contrast at σ 0 / 0.1 (Limitation 8 says it is missing) costs no new simulation and should be added.

**B-W4. The intervals in the online study have little meaning because the orderings share instances (moderate).** We checked the stream files. In S2, all 20 orderings use the **identical 1,500 post-shift instances**, permuted. In S1/S3, two orderings share about 1,080–1,094 of 1,500 post-shift instances (the union over orderings is 2,096). The "95 % intervals over orderings" therefore measure order effects on a nearly fixed sample, not sampling variability of slip under shift. They should not be read as CIs for the late-window slip rate. The text says "intervals over orderings only". It should also say that the instances are shared, and an object-cluster bootstrap should be added for the physical-feedback rows (the H3 rows already have one).

**B-W5. Conditional miscoverage is large and concentrated, and the obvious remedy is not tried (moderate).** The lowest friction quintile slips at 0.216 for the learned model, more than twice α. The plug-in's profile is much flatter (0.086 → 0.029), and CSF-1's is extreme (0.48 → 0). For grasping, the low-friction objects are the ones that matter. Mondrian or group-conditional CRC on μ̂ bins (deployable, since μ̂ is observed) would give per-bin guarantees at little cost and can be evaluated on the existing replays. The flat plug-in profile is also a further, unreported argument for the plug-in over the learner. Mention it in Section 5.2.

**B-W6. Labelling inconsistencies (minor).** In Table 2 the friction-prior σ_p 0.2 row is labelled "edge rule". But −0.0299 is the learned arm **on the refined grid** against the prior on the refined grid (`results/v2/b_analysis_edge.json`: learned RE 0.4442), which is what the shrunk-plug-in rows call "same grid". Under the shrunk rows' "edge rule" convention (learned on the primary grid), the value would be 1 − 0.4567/0.4312 = **−0.059**. Use one convention throughout. Also state that the Clopper–Pearson upper limit 0.02115 is one-sided at 98.75 % (the Bonferroni-split level). A reader computing the usual 95 % limit gets 0.0180 < 0.02 and would conclude that the gate passed.

**B-W7. Multiplicity across rounds (minor).** Three amendment rounds were registered after earlier read-outs. Most second- and third-round rows are unadjusted, and three inferiority rows have upper bounds within 0.02 of zero. The paper flags all of this, which is good. The abstract nonetheless quotes two of them (the σ 0.4 shrunk result and the −0.3152 refined sub-pool) next to the confirmatory D, without the "unadjusted, post hoc" label. Add it.

**B-W8. Effect-size metric (minor).** D is a ratio of mean signed RE. Slipped instances contribute negative excess, so an arm that slips more gets a smaller RE. In H2a the probe arm slips *less* (0.083 vs 0.102), so this is conservative for the paper's claim, which is good. But for contrasts where the method slips more (μ ∧ amp refined; σ 0.4 shrunk), D is not comparable. Matched-slip D, or RE restricted to non-slipped instances, should accompany those rows.

### Questions for the authors
1. Can a one-outcome-per-grasp CRC be evaluated on the existing replays by subsampling one random grid force per calibration instance? This would directly show what the certificate costs on hardware.
2. What do group-conditional CRC on μ̂ tertiles and its lowest-quintile slip look like?
3. Does the online tracker's late-window slip CI change when instances are resampled by object rather than by ordering?

### Actionable revisions (Reviewer B)
1. Add a single-outcome calibration variant (or a clear theorem-level discussion), and temper "certified" in the title and abstract to "simulation-certified".
2. State that edge-rule and refined-grid rows are outside the exact CRC theorem, or move the grid choice to the selection split.
3. Add refined-grid bootstrap contrasts for learned vs plug-in at σ 0 / 0.1, and a group-conditional CRC (μ̂ bins) row with quintile slip.
4. Disclose instance sharing across orderings, and add an object-cluster bootstrap to the physical-feedback rows.
5. Fix the σ_p 0.2 "edge rule" label (−0.0299 vs −0.059), and state the CP level (98.75 %, one-sided).
6. Label the abstract's secondary numbers as unadjusted, post hoc secondaries.

**Score (Reviewer B): 5 / 10 (borderline).** Statistically sound and unusually transparent. Every number we checked reproduces, and the attribution result (the plug-in is enough) is valuable. It falls short because the guarantee that gives the paper its name needs counterfactual simulator replays, the deployable online route carries no per-stratum finite-sample statement, and the effect size is set by assumed σ and a constructed friction distribution.
