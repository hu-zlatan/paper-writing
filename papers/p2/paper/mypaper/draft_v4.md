# When Does a Friction-Onset Probe Pay Off? Conformal Slip-Risk Control of Grip Hold Force, Mapped in Simulation

## Abstract

**When does probing friction before a certified grasp pay off?** In our simulation, only under three conditions together: friction cannot be predicted from the object's appearance, probe noise is low (assumed σ 0.2, not 0.4), and friction varies widely across objects. Even then the saving is modest: 0.66 N (9.3 %) of hold force for a one-line plug-in and 1.01 N (14 %) for a learned model. The probe's own force-time is repaid only in holds longer than 17.7–27.1 s, and we did not simulate slip over holds that long. Our method holds a fixed preload, then runs a ramp-down probe that reads friction at incipient-slip onset. A Coulomb plug-in (or a learned model) turns that reading into a hold force, and conformal risk control on the binary physical-slip loss calibrates it per disturbance stratum, so that the expected slip rate is at most α (a marginal, in-expectation bound). In pre-registered, Holm-corrected tests on fresh objects, the plug-in lowers relative excess hold force against the strongest friction-blind conformal baseline (D = 0.43). Which component earns the saving depends on the cost metric. On relative excess the learned model is equivalent to the plug-in; on absolute excess (post hoc) it saves about as much again. The probe-versus-learner attribution is therefore unresolved. A family friction prior beats both tactile arms when friction follows the visible family, and slip concentrates on low-friction objects. Online binary-feedback tracking returns slip near α after a shift, but its efficiency against oracle recalibration is inconclusive.

## 1 Introduction

A robot holding an object has to choose a grip force. Too much force wastes actuation and loads the object for the whole hold. Too little lets it slip. Humans scale grip to friction after brief contact \cite{johansson1984roles} and adjust again when friction changes \cite{willemet2024grip}. Tactile controllers react to incipient slip \cite{dong2019maintaining, veiga2020grip, berman2025autonomous}, estimate friction online \cite{khamis2021real, niu2026synchronized} or learn a gentle-grasp margin \cite{boonstra2024estimate}, but none of them states a distribution-free bound on the slip rate that results. Our problem is narrower than minimal-force grasping. We choose the *hold* force that follows a fixed, high-force probe protocol, and we require that the physical slip rate of that hold force be at most α. The tactile signal is a ramp-down probe that reads friction from the force at incipient-slip onset. The certificate comes from distribution-free conformal risk control (CRC) on the binary physical-slip loss. The comparator is the strongest non-tactile conformal rule we could build.

**The answer in brief.** We asked when such a probe pays off. In simulation, it pays off in hold force when three conditions hold together. First, friction must not be predictable from what the robot sees: when friction follows the visible object family, a one-number family prior beats both tactile arms (matched D −0.58). Second, the probe must be quiet: at the assumed onset noise σ 0.2 the plug-in's relative-excess saving is D 0.43, but at σ 0.4 it reverses (−0.21). Third, friction must vary widely: as the friction range narrows, the friction-blind baseline catches up and the saving reverses. When all three hold, the saving is modest in newtons. Against the baseline, the plug-in holds 0.66 N (9.3 %) less and the learned model 1.01 N (14 %) less. Counting the whole grasp, the probe's extra phases are repaid in force-time only for holds longer than 17.7 s (learned model) to 27.1 s (plug-in). Our simulated disturbance window lasts 0.6 s, so whether the certified force stays safe over holds that long is untested (Limitation L9). Which component carries the saving depends on the cost metric. By the registered metric, relative excess, one trained learned model adds nothing over the plug-in at σ 0.2. By absolute excess in newtons (post hoc), it saves about as much again as the probe does. We therefore regard the probe-versus-learner attribution as unresolved, not settled in favour of the probe.

**Why certify the slip rate rather than lower the average force?** A grip controller is judged by its worst common failure, and an object that slips is usually a dropped object. Controllers that add a fixed safety margin to an estimated friction limit \cite{khamis2021real, johansson1984roles} implicitly trade force for safety. The designer sets that margin by hand, and its slip rate on a new population of objects stays unknown until objects are dropped. A calibrated statement of the form "on objects drawn like these, the hold force slips in at most a fraction α of grasps" turns the trade-off into one number the user sets. What that statement costs depends on what the force rule knows. Suppose the rule knows mass, commanded acceleration and disturbance type but not friction. Its calibrated multiplier must then be large enough for nearly all of the slippery objects in the pool. With friction drawn between 0.3 and 1.0, high-friction objects are held well above the force they need: on our fresh objects the friction-blind baseline's mean relative excess at matched slip is 0.75. Friction is exactly what a tactile probe can supply, and it is the only quantity our rules are not given. Every rule in this paper receives the true mass, the commanded acceleration and the disturbance stratum.

**The probe is not free.** To read friction from the incipient-slip onset, the gripper must squeeze, ramp the force down until the object starts to slip, and return to a safe force, all before the hold begins. In short holds this probe-and-return sequence costs more force-time than it saves. Over a 1 s hold the integrated grip force is 22.2 N·s for a grasp that skips the probe and 39.1 N·s for the learned probe model. Over a 30 s hold the order flips (229 versus 216 N·s). Whether touch "pays off" is therefore not a yes-or-no question. It depends on probe noise, on how widely friction varies, on whether friction can be guessed from appearance, and on how long the object is held. This paper maps those conditions under one certificate, so that tactile and non-tactile rules are always compared at the same certified slip level.

**A fair comparator.** CRC can certify any force rule from a held-out calibration set \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal, lei2017distribution}. A rule that cannot see friction, however, must cover the whole friction range with one calibrated multiplier. The fair comparator for a tactile method is therefore the *strongest* friction-blind conformal baseline, chosen beforehand on a disjoint selection split. On that split, CSF-1 (mean relative excess 0.769) beat a Mondrian variant (0.829) and a learned proprioceptive model (0.834). Where friction can be predicted from the visible object family, we also compare against a deployable family prior.

**One cost picture.** We report three cost measures side by side, because each answers a different question. *Relative excess* (registered) asks how far above its own need each grasp is held. *Absolute excess* (post hoc) asks how many newtons above need are spent on average. *Integrated force-time* (closed-form accounting) asks what the whole grasp costs, probe included. The probe wins clearly on the first measure, moderately on the second, and on the third only for long holds. The learned model ties the plug-in on the first and beats it on the second. Section 5.2 puts the three side by side.

**Research questions.** We ask *when a short tactile probe makes a distribution-free physical-slip bound cheaper in hold force, and which component carries the saving*: (i) does the probe lower hold force at certified physical slip ≤ α (in simulation, marginal over the pool, in expectation), and is a learned model needed? (ii) Under which conditions (probe noise, friction range, a friction-informative prior, hold duration) does the saving hold or disappear? (iii) Does calibration recover under shift when the only feedback is whether the object slipped?

**Contributions.**
- **(C-i) Method.** A protocol in which a friction-uninformative preload leaves the ramp-down onset as the only friction channel. Per-stratum CRC on the monotonised physical binary slip loss (the label rule is not exactly monotone, 8 of 800) turns a one-line Coulomb plug-in on that onset into a rule with marginal expected slip ≤ α in simulation.
- **(C-ii) Confirmatory evaluation on fresh objects.** Five pre-registered, Holm-corrected decisions. The plug-in beats the baseline (C1). One trained learned model is equivalent to the plug-in in relative excess at σ 0.2 on the registered matched-slip route (C2). The earlier learned-model result replicates (C3). On a friction-correlated pool, a family prior beats both tactile arms (C4, C5).
- **(C-ii′) One cost picture.** Post hoc re-reads of the same replays (absolute excess and hold force in newtons, slip by friction tercile), placed next to the registered relative metric and the closed-form force-time accounting. Together they show that the size of the saving and its attribution depend on the cost metric.
- **(C-iii) Boundary map.** A σ band, a friction-width sweep, a break-even against a simulated no-probe grasp, a 200-re-split check of the certificate, and an online binary-feedback study under three shifts.

**Outcome summary.** Three of the five confirmatory predictions are met. The two rows without a registered prediction (C4, C5) are not superior, and their intervals lie below 0. Two earlier pre-registered decisions were not confirmed (learned versus plug-in, and label validity), and online efficiency is inconclusive. Every caveat is stated once, in Section 6.2 (L1–L11), and the rest of the paper refers to it there. Section 2 places the work. Section 3 defines the problem, protocol, force rules and calibration, Section 4 the simulator, pools and statistics, and Section 5 the results. Section 6 says what the results do and do not license. Appendix A collects supporting rows from earlier rounds.

## 2 Related Work

**Friction reading and tactile grip control.** Reading friction from incipient slip is an established technique \cite{marconi1996incipient}. Reviews treat incipient slip as the key friction cue \cite{chen2018tactile, romeo2020methods}, and human grip adapts to friction \cite{johansson1984roles, willemet2024grip}. Robotic controllers detect slip and tighten \cite{james2020slip, grover2022detect, ayral2026reactive}, keep grasps within slipping bounds \cite{dong2019maintaining}, or estimate friction and add a safety margin \cite{khamis2021real, niu2026synchronized}. Others learn incipient-slip margins or force policies \cite{boonstra2024estimate, kuo2025tracing, feng2026tactilereflex, lach2024zero}, or stabilise grasps with adaptive or independent-finger control \cite{almohammed2022switched, veiga2020grip}. Pre-grasp force can also be chosen from prior experience \cite{shang2026exp}, which relates to our family-prior result. None of these methods gives a coverage guarantee on the slip rate. Our probe borrows the friction-reading idea and adds the calibration layer that turns it into a certified rule.

**Slip margins and what our plug-in is.** The classical rule, both in human grip \cite{johansson1984roles} and in robotic friction-estimating controllers such as \cite{khamis2021real}, is to hold at the estimated slip limit plus a safety margin. Our probe plug-in follows the same rule. Because the onset force is read at the same gravity load, mass cancels, and the plug-in holds at a calibrated multiple of the measured onset force scaled by the commanded acceleration (up to a clip on μ̂). The difference lies in how the margin is set. In such controllers it is a design constant. Here it is the CRC multiplier, set from a calibration set so that the expected slip rate on new objects from the same distribution is at most α. On the first-round pool, a margin tuned on the same calibration data lands at the same operating point (Appendix A). What CRC adds is therefore the finite-sample guarantee, not a better controller. We did not reproduce published controllers (L7).

**Vision and experience priors.** A friction-informative prior need not come from touch. Vision-language models conditioned on retrieved grasp experience predict a minimum feasible force from a single image \cite{shang2026exp}, and risk margins can be computed from a friction distribution before contact \cite{enwerem2026firmgrasp}. Our family prior CSF-fam is the simplest member of this class, with one friction value per visible family. Our C4 / C5 result is what one should expect whenever such a prior is sharp. The natural next comparator, a rule that combines a prior with the probe, was not run (L2).

**Robust grasping under uncertainty.** Force optimisation under parameter uncertainty \cite{cloutier2018examining}, control barrier functions \cite{cortez2019control, kim2024robust}, friction-informed risk margins \cite{enwerem2026firmgrasp} and belief parameterisations \cite{enwerem2026variational} bound grasp risk relative to a model and its assumed uncertainty set. Our bound needs no friction model. In exchange it is marginal and needs calibration data, so the two families of guarantees are complementary rather than competing.

**Conformal prediction and risk control.** Conformal methods give finite-sample guarantees under exchangeability \cite{vovk2005algorithmic, romano2019conformalized, angelopoulos2023conformal, bian2023training, ding2023class}. CRC extends them to monotone losses \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal}, non-monotone losses \cite{angelopoulos2026conformal} and high-probability control \cite{angelopoulos2025learn}. Online variants track coverage under shift \cite{gibbs2021adaptive, gibbs2022conformal, angelopoulos2023conformala, angelopoulos2024online, farinhas2023non, ge2024stochastic}. In robotics, conformal methods certify decisions, planning, verification and perception \cite{lekeufack2024conformal, lindemann2025formal, dixit2022adaptive, marques2026particle, lindemann2023conformal, ren2023robots, huang2023conformal, strawn2023conformal, zhang2024bayesian, zhao2024conformalized, kumar2026learnable}. None of them calibrates a contact outcome as a binary loss in force space.

**Conditional coverage.** Marginal guarantees can hide systematic under-coverage of sub-groups. Mondrian or class-conditional calibration \cite{vovk2005algorithmic, ding2023class} calibrates separately within groups, at the cost of fewer calibration points per group. Training-conditional analyses \cite{bian2023training} separate the guarantee over the calibration draw from the guarantee for a fixed calibration set. We calibrate per known disturbance stratum, which amounts to a Mondrian split by stratum, and we use Learn-then-Test (LTT) for a high-probability version. We do not calibrate per object family or per friction level, and Section 5.4 shows where that matters (L4).

**Novelty.** Onset-based friction reading, CRC, LTT and quantile tracking are all used as published. What is new is (i) a protocol in which the onset is the only friction channel, so that its value can be isolated; (ii) a marginal bound on the *physical* slip rate of the resulting hold force, in simulation; and (iii) confirmatory tests and a cost picture that show where the saving holds, how large it is in newtons and force-time, and where it disappears.

## 3 Problem Formulation and Method

### 3.1 Problem

An instance x comprises an object (family, geometry, mass m, friction μ), a pose offset and a disturbance stratum d ∈ {D1, …, D4}. Slip is a tangential displacement above 2 mm, a rotation above 3° or loss of contact. The minimal no-slip hold force F\*(x) is bracketed by 11-replay log-space bisection on [0.1, 100] N into F\*_lo, F\*_hi and F\*_mid = √(F\*_lo F\*_hi). These labels exist only in simulation. With relative excess RE(F) = E_x[(F(x) − F\*_mid(x))/F\*_mid(x)] of the hold force, the task is

$$\min_F \ \mathrm{RE}(F) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbb{P}(\mathrm{slip\ at\ } F(x)) \le \alpha .$$

Two rules are compared by D(a vs b) = 1 − RE(a)/RE(b); D > 0 means that a needs less excess force than b. The registered objective is relative. Section 4 defines the absolute counterpart, AE, and the integrated force-time used in the cost picture of Section 5.2.

### 3.2 Grasp protocol and friction-onset probe

Every instance receives the same preload F₀ = 32.7 N, which encodes neither m nor μ. The gripper lifts and holds, squeezes F₀ → 1.5 F₀ → F₀, and then runs the **ramp-down probe**. The force falls at a fixed rate until a touch-only detector on the pad shear/normal ratio reports incipient slip, which gives F_onset and μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset). Simulated touch is not clean. The taxel model adds per-taxel multiplicative and additive noise and a per-instance channel gain. On the first-round pool, the resulting friction estimate at no added noise has log error log μ̂ − log μ with mean +0.3186 and SD 0.1103; the calibrated multiplier absorbs a constant log bias. On top of this detector we add onset noise, log F_onset + σε with ε ~ N(0, 1), σ ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.4}. σ is therefore added noise, not total noise; σ 0.2 is the primary setting and is assumed (L1). The grip then ramps to the chosen hold force F, and a slip during the probe counts as a slip. The protocol is fixed. The squeeze peak 1.5 F₀ exceeds every instance's F\*_mid, while the preload exceeds nearly all of them but not every one (a few calibration and test instances need slightly more than F₀). The probe induces micro-slip by design, and the method chooses only the hold force (L6). The **no-probe grasp** uses the same preload and skips the squeeze and probe phases. It is the reference for the force-time accounting.

### 3.3 Force rules

Every rule has the form F(x; λ) = min(F_max, F_base(x) e^λ), with λ set by calibration. Every rule receives the true mass m, the commanded acceleration a_cmd and the disturbance stratum d (L10). a_cmd is the lift acceleration in D1, the peak shake acceleration in D2, and zero for the external disturbances D3 and D4, whose amplitude the robot does not know. Only friction is unknown.
- **Probe plug-in:** F_base = m(g + a_cmd)/(2μ̂). With μ̂ = m g/(2 F_onset), mass cancels and F_base = F_onset (1 + a_cmd/g), up to the clip of μ̂ to [0.1, 3.0]. The plug-in is therefore a calibrated multiple of the measured onset force.
- **Shrunk plug-in:** log μ̂_s = a + b log μ̂, fitted by ordinary least squares on the selection split only and frozen from the previous round; at σ 0.2, a = −0.3858 and b = 0.7397. This two-parameter correction of the raw onset estimate sits between the plug-in and the learned model.
- **Learned probe model (option):** a gradient-boosted quantile predictor on proprioception, static touch, squeeze and onset features. Its fit is unchanged here, but it is reproduced rather than loaded. The first round stored predictions, not models, so the seed-0 model is re-fitted deterministically (fixed random state) from its stored configuration on the same training rows. It is accepted only if it reproduces the stored first-round log-predictions to within 1e-5, which an assertion in the code checks. Every fresh-pool and correlated-pool row uses this one fit of training seed 0.
- **Friction-blind baseline CSF-1:** F_base = m(g + a_cmd)/(2μ̄) with one fixed friction value μ̄ = √(0.3 · 1.0), the geometric mean of the design friction range (hard-coded; e^λ absorbs any constant). The selection split chose the baseline *type* (CSF-1 against a Mondrian variant and a learned proprioceptive model) before any calibration or test data were accessed. It did not choose μ̄.
- **Family prior CSF-fam (deployable, non-tactile):** the same formula with one μ̄ per visible family. Each μ̄ is the geometric-mean true μ of the family's 50 selection objects in the correlated pool, which are disjoint from calibration and test: F1 0.379, F2 0.496, F3 0.677, F4 0.852.
- **Earlier-round supporting arms (Appendix A):** an onset-only learner; an oracle-side friction prior μ̃ = clip(μ e^{σ_p ε_p}, 0.1, 3.0); the probe plus a tuned per-stratum margin; and reactive slip-tightening (a registered negative control).

### 3.4 Calibration on the physical binary loss

For calibration instance i, the loss is L_i(λ) = 𝟙{physical slip in a direct replay at F(x_i; λ)}. It is monotonised on the grid Λ as L̃_i(λ) = max_{λ′ ≥ λ} L_i(λ′). Since L̃_i ≥ L_i, a bound on L̃ also bounds physical slip. An *unavoidable* instance, one that slips even at F_max, counts as a slip at every λ in calibration and test. It is excluded from RE because it has no finite F\*_mid.

**CRC (primary).** Per stratum, with R̃_n(λ) the mean of L̃_i over n calibration instances,

$$\hat\lambda = \min\Big\{\lambda \in \Lambda : \frac{n \tilde R_n(\lambda) + 1}{n + 1} \le \alpha\Big\}.$$

By \cite{angelopoulos2022conformal}, an exchangeable test instance then has E[slip] ≤ α. The bound is marginal over instances and friction and holds in expectation over the calibration draw. It is not conditional on a stratum realisation, an object family or a friction level (L4).

**LTT (secondary).** λ is chosen so that the slip risk is ≤ α with probability ≥ 1 − δ, δ = 0.1, per stratum \cite{angelopoulos2025learn, bian2023training}.

**Why the physical loss.** The label route (split conformal on bisection labels \cite{romano2019conformalized}) transfers to physical slip only if slip is monotone in F. The monotonicity gate failed: 8 of 800 instances were non-monotone (registered one-sided 98.75 % Clopper–Pearson upper limit 0.02115 > 0.02; 7 of 8 in D2), so the label route is secondary. The level of the test decided the gate. The count is small, and a less strict level could have passed it. Calibrating directly on the physical outcome avoids the question altogether: the monotonised loss is monotone by construction and dominates the raw slip indicator.

**Deployability.** Offline CRC needs a replay of each calibration grasp at every grid force, so it is a simulation procedure. The online tracker needs only one binary outcome per grasp (L5).

```
Algorithm 1: probe, then calibrate (per arm, per stratum d)
1  every instance: preload F0, squeeze, ramp-down probe -> F_onset (+ noise sigma); F_base(x) uses it
2  for calibration i, lambda in Lambda: L[i,lambda] <- slip of replay at min(F_max, F_base(x_i) e^lambda)
3  Ltilde[i,lambda] <- max_{lambda' >= lambda} L[i,lambda']
4  lambda_hat <- first lambda with (sum_i Ltilde[i,lambda] + 1)/(n + 1) <= alpha
5  deploy: probe, then command F = min(F_max, F_base(x) e^lambda_hat)
```

### 3.5 Online calibration from binary feedback

Score-space quantile tracking \cite{angelopoulos2023conformala} updates one log-force offset per stratum, q_{t+1} = q_t + η(slip_t − α), with η = 0.03412 (frozen) and anti-windup at F_max. We track in score space rather than with the level update of \cite{gibbs2021adaptive} because score space tolerates scores beyond the calibration support after a friction drop. The reference schemes are a decaying step \cite{angelopoulos2024online}, frozen calibration and oracle recalibration. The tracker uses exactly the feedback a deployed gripper has, whether the object slipped, and needs no replay.

## 4 Experimental Setup

**Simulator and objects.** We use MuJoCo 4.0.0, a parallel-jaw gripper and one touch grid per pad (on tactile simulation see \cite{wang2022tacto, akinola2025tacsl, leins2025hydroelastictouch, luu2025manifeel, birtalan2026impact}). The families are F1 boxes, F2 cylinders, F3 ellipsoids / capsules and F4 off-centre-of-mass composites; F5 thin plates / disks appear only under shift. The summed pad normal reading differs from F₀ by at most 0.1597 N, and no instance slipped during the preload. The simulation step is 2 ms. Each pad carries an 8 × 8 touch grid, and the incipient-slip detector sees only these taxels (shear/normal ratio and micro-displacement), never the true friction. Contact is rigid MuJoCo contact with Coulomb friction; no soft-pad, hydroelastic or viscoelastic effects are modelled (L1, L9).

**Pools.** The first-round pool draws μ log-uniform on [0.3, 1.0], independently of every non-tactile input, and m log-uniform on [0.05, 1.0] kg (2,000 calibration / 2,800 test instances). The **fresh pool (independent friction)** repeats this design with new objects (500 calibration / 700 test objects) and carries C1–C3, S1–S3, S6 and S7. The **friction-correlated pool** draws friction in narrow, mostly non-overlapping family bands (500 / 700 objects) and carries C4, C5 and S4. Every object appears once in each of the four strata, so a pool of 700 test objects gives 2,800 test instances. Calibration and test objects never overlap. A separate selection split, used only to choose the baseline type, fit the shrinkage, set the family prior and centre the force grids, overlaps neither.

**Labels and grid.** For every instance the simulator replays the full grasp deterministically at any commanded hold force. The minimal no-slip force is bracketed by bisection, and each calibration and test instance is replayed at every point of an 81-point log-force grid per arm and stratum. The grid is centred on a quantile fixed from selection rows only and capped at F_max = 100 N. These replays give the physical slip outcome L_i(λ) used by CRC and by every test-slip figure; no calibration or test label enters the grid. Unavoidable instances (Section 3.4) are rare: one calibration and one test instance on the fresh pool and one calibration instance on the correlated pool (post hoc count). The convention therefore cannot move any reported row by more than one instance in 2,800.

**Strata and shifts.** Stratum D1 is lift acceleration (1–10 m/s²) and D2 is shake (5–20 mm amplitude at 2–5 Hz). D3 is a lateral pull on the object and D4 an added payload; both are ramped in over 0.1 s and applied during the 0.6 s disturbance window that follows the ramp to the hold force. The stratum is known to every rule and is the calibration group; D3 and D4 amplitudes are not known to the rules. The online shifts are friction (μ × 0.6), family (new F5) and amplitude (× 1.5), on streams of T = 2,000 steps with the shift from step 501 and 20 orderings.

**Statistics.** CI\*\* is a two-sided 97.5 % object-cluster bootstrap interval (2,000 draws, all valid; calibration and test objects resampled within family, λ̂ refitted in each). It is shown for display and does not decide the confirmatory tests. Two comparison routes are used. On the *CRC route* each arm is evaluated at its own certified λ̂, so arms may differ in realised slip. On the *test-slip-matched route* each stratum's λ̂ index is shifted by up to ±8 grid steps and RE is interpolated at a realised test slip of 0.10. This route compares arms at equal realised slip, but it uses test outcomes, so it is a frontier comparison rather than a deployable certified rule. It is the registered route for C1, C2, C4 and C5; C3 is on the CRC route. The smallest effect size of interest (SESOI) is 0.10. Bootstrap p-values are p = min(1, 2 · min(share of draws ≤ 0, share ≥ 0)). As registered, C1, C3, C4 and C5 declare superiority iff the Holm-adjusted p over the five confirmatory tests is < 0.05 and the point estimate is > 0. C2 declares equivalence iff both one-sided TOST p-values at ±0.10 pass Holm. Secondary equivalence statements use the same TOST criterion, unadjusted. A grid-boundary check (no λ̂ at the first or last grid index in any stratum, which would make a row uninterpretable) passed for every arm and row of the confirmatory round. First-round core decisions used Bonferroni over 4, and its 52 secondaries Holm within family (no verdict changed). S1–S7 and later-round rows are unadjusted (L8).

**Cost measures and post hoc rows (PH).** After the confirmatory read-out, reviewers asked for forces in newtons and for slip by friction level. These rows re-read the existing confirmatory replays. They are report-only, were not pre-registered and decide nothing. They use the same split, the same per-stratum CRC and the same matched-slip recipe, with 2,000 object-cluster draws (seed 2027). Absolute excess is AE(F) = E_x[F(x) − F\*_mid(x)] in newtons on avoidable test instances, and D_abs(a vs b) = 1 − AE(a)/AE(b) on either route. Hold-force savings are differences of mean hold force against CSF-1 at each arm's CRC point. Slip by friction tercile splits the 2,800 fresh test instances at the tercile edges of true μ (0.4457 and 0.6553). Integrated force-time (S6) is hold force × hold time plus the fixed phase integrals of the protocol. Relative and absolute excess answer different questions. RE asks how far above its own need each grasp is held, so instances with small F\* (light or high-friction) weigh most. AE asks how many newtons above need are spent on average, so heavy or slippery instances dominate. An arm that corrects the onset estimate mainly on heavy or slippery objects therefore gains more on AE than on RE. Neither includes the probe's fixed force-time, which only the integrated measure counts.

**Figures.** Figure 1(b) and Figure 2 are data figures rendered from the analysis output of the confirmatory round. Every value in their captions and panels is one of the values in Tables 1–3 or in Section 5.2, and we checked them against these tables. Figure 1(a) is a schematic with real phase integrals.

**Deviations.** (1) The CRC route was written after the monotonicity counts were seen, before any predictor existed. (2) A first-round grid floor was handled by a post hoc refined grid (earlier-round rows only). (3) The first-round pre-registration misstates the selection objects once; the code uses 150–199. (4) Later-round analyses were registered after earlier results were seen and cannot change a first-round decision. (5) The registered physical-replay online oracle was not produced. (6) The confirmatory protocol was registered before any of its code was written or run; its decision code was committed before any confirmatory replay and run once. (7) C4 / C5 had no registered prediction; the rule defines only superiority there. (8) The break-even S6 was registered as a "measured break-even … from the replay's commanded profile". It is delivered as a closed-form computation (hold force × hold time plus fixed phase integrals), whose squeeze term uses the mean measured pad-normal reading during the squeeze rather than the commanded profile; it has no interval. (9) S3 was listed among secondary rows but carried a registered directional prediction (D shrinks with w, with D ≤ 0 expected near w 0.25); we report its outcome (Section 5.3). (10) The PH rows above were added after the read-out at reviewers' request and are not part of the registered analysis.

### 5.1 Confirmatory tests on fresh objects

**On 700 fresh test objects at σ 0.2, all three registered predictions hold. On the friction-correlated pool, the family prior beats both tactile arms.**

*C2 (CONF).* On the registered matched-slip route at σ 0.2, the learned model is equivalent to the plug-in within ±0.10: D = 0.01117 [−0.05552, 0.05686], and both one-sided TOST p-values are < 0.0005 and pass Holm. On the CRC route the evidence is weaker: −0.06291 [−0.1226, 0.08396]. Here two criteria disagree. The unadjusted TOST p is 0.037, below 0.05, but the 97.5 % interval does not lie inside ±0.10. We therefore do not claim equivalence on the CRC route. C2's equivalence holds on the registered matched route, for relative excess, and for one trained model (L10, L11). At its CRC point the learned model holds less mean absolute force than the plug-in (6.115 versus 6.463 N) and slips less (0.08107 versus 0.09786). Section 5.2 takes this up.

*C3 (CONF).* On the CRC route the learned model beats CSF-1, D = 0.4135 [0.3578, 0.4903]. This replicates the first-round pre-registered result (0.4168 [0.3484, 0.4778]; seeds 1–4: 0.4343 / 0.4197 / 0.3932 / 0.4494).

### 5.2 One cost picture: relative excess, absolute excess and force-time

**The probe saves clearly in relative excess, moderately in newtons, and in whole-grasp force-time only for long holds. The learned model ties the plug-in in relative excess and beats it in newtons, so the probe-versus-learner attribution is unresolved across metrics.**

Table 2 sets out, per fresh-pool arm, the three cost measures defined in Section 4. The registered metric is relative excess at matched slip (CSF-1 0.7518, plug-in 0.4275, shrunk 0.3797, learned 0.4227). The post hoc metric is absolute excess at matched slip (CSF-1 2.359 N, plug-in 1.800 N, shrunk 1.386 N, learned 1.320 N). The accounting measure is integrated force-time over the whole grasp at the CRC point (S6, DESC). Read top to bottom, the three measures tell one consistent story about the probe and two different stories about the learner.

*The probe against the friction-blind baseline.* All three measures agree on the sign. Measured in relative excess, the plug-in's matched saving is D 0.4314 (C1). In absolute excess (PH) it is D_abs 0.2372 [0.1401, 0.3328], and on the CRC route 0.2670 [0.09526, 0.3705]. In hold force at the CRC point (PH) the plug-in holds 0.66 N (9.3 %) less than CSF-1, the shrunk plug-in 0.94 N less and the learned model 1.01 N (14.2 %) less. These savings are modest, and they must be set against the probe protocol's extra phases, which add force-time to every grasp: squeeze 12.91, ramp-down 1.444, return 0.3480 and re-hold 3.27 N·s, on top of the shared preload of 13.08 N·s.

*The learner against the plug-in.* Here the measures disagree. In relative excess the learned model is equivalent to the plug-in (C2, D 0.01117). In absolute excess at matched slip it beats the plug-in by 0.2667 [0.1980, 0.3196], about as much as the plug-in gains over CSF-1 on that metric. The CRC-route absolute rows agree in sign (learned vs plug-in 0.1911 [0.1194, 0.3349]; learned vs CSF-1 0.4071 [0.3283, 0.5087]). A two-parameter shrinkage of the raw onset estimate comes close to the learner: shrunk plug-in against learned model gives −0.05046 [−0.1226, 0.01035]. The C2 equivalence is therefore a property of the relative metric. By relative excess the probe carries the saving; by absolute excess the learner, or a shrinkage of the raw onset estimate, adds about as much again. We do not resolve which metric is the right cost. The registered one weights each grasp by its own need, and the post hoc one weights newtons, so the attribution of the saving to the probe rather than the learner is unresolved across metrics (L11). Section 5.4 adds a third observation: the learner and the shrunk plug-in concentrate their slips more on low-friction objects than the plug-in does.

*Whole-grasp force-time.* Integrated force-time is computed in closed form (hold force × hold time plus the fixed phase integrals), not measured over simulated long holds as the registration's wording ("measured break-even") suggested (Deviation 8). The phase integrals follow the commanded profiles, except the squeeze, which uses the mean measured pad-normal reading. The hold forces, and the no-probe grasp's slip and calibrated multiplier, come from the replays, and no interval is attached. The simulated no-probe grasp slips at 0.08714 and holds 7.125 N (λ̂ at grid index 41 in all strata). Integrated force-time at T = 1 / 5 / 30 s is 22.20 / 50.70 / 228.8 N·s without the probe, 39.10 / 63.57 / 216.4 for the learned model, 39.47 / 65.32 / 226.9 for the plug-in and 40.17 / 68.67 / 246.8 for CSF-1 with the probe. Break-even is at 17.74 s (learned) and 27.07 s (plug-in); CSF-1 with the probe never breaks even. For 1 s and 5 s holds the no-probe grasp uses the least force-time, and the fixed preload and squeeze dominate. Whether the certified force stays safe over holds as long as the break-even is untested (L9). The break-even is an accounting of force, not a measured long-hold outcome.

*On the correlated pool.* The family prior also wins on absolute excess (plug-in vs CSF-fam −0.6644 [−0.8376, −0.5043], learned vs CSF-fam −0.3236 [−0.4537, −0.1945], matched). The C4 / C5 conclusion therefore does not depend on the metric.

### 5.3 Boundary map: noise and friction range

**The saving shrinks and then reverses as probe noise rises or as the friction range narrows.**

*σ band (S1, SEC; Fig. 2a).* D(plug-in vs CSF-1) is 0.6486 [0.6100, 0.6733] / 0.4314 / −0.2065 [−0.3280, −0.1112] at σ 0.1 / 0.2 / 0.4. At σ 0.4 the plug-in's test slip is 0.1014, above α. Learned versus plug-in is −0.1076 [−0.1819, −0.02922] at σ 0.1 (learned worse; TOST p 0.5445) and 0.3238 [0.2778, 0.3817] at σ 0.4 (learned better), so C2's equivalence is a σ 0.2 result. The reversal at σ 0.4 is the plug-in's alone. On the test-slip-matched route the learned model's RE is 0.2926 / 0.4227 / 0.6134 at σ 0.1 / 0.2 / 0.4 against CSF-1's 0.7518 (the plug-in's is 0.2642 / 0.4275 / 0.9071). On the CRC route the learned model still beats CSF-1 at σ 0.4 (0.1675 [0.1064, 0.2560]). At high probe noise, then, the learner and not the raw probe estimate carries the saving, so this row also argues that the probe-versus-learner attribution depends on conditions (L11). An earlier round found the opposite at σ 0.4 on the first-round pool, where a shrunk plug-in beat the learned model (−0.1395 [−0.1832, −0.01264]); the cause of the difference is not tested.

*Friction-width sweep (S3, SEC, matched route; Fig. 2b).* We restrict the fresh pool to centred friction windows of relative log-width w. D(plug-in vs CSF-1) then runs 0.4314 → 0.2414 → −0.1094 → −0.6967 for w = 1.0 → 0.75 → 0.5 → 0.25 (CI\*\* [−0.2383, 0.01393] at w 0.5; [−0.9507, −0.4238] at w 0.25, with 134 calibration / 179 test objects). The learned model gives 0.4377 / 0.2793 / −0.01792 / −0.5039. The sweep crosses 0 between w 0.75 and w 0.5. The point estimates meet the registered directional prediction (D shrinks with w, with D ≤ 0 expected near w 0.25); the sweep has no confirmatory test (L3). CSF-1's matched RE falls from 0.7518 to 0.2307 while the plug-in stays between 0.3915 and 0.4275, so the decline comes mainly from CSF-1 improving. We hypothesise, but did not test, that this reflects friction spread relative to probe noise. The trend matches the direction of the earlier mid-friction, mid-amplitude sub-pool (Appendix A), whose sign is grid-dependent and for which no mechanism is claimed.

The third boundary, hold duration, is the force-time break-even of Section 5.2 (Fig. 2c).

### 5.4 How the certificate behaves

**Over 200 re-splits of the fresh pool, mean slip stays below α for every arm, but the bound is marginal: single strata, one object family and the lowest-friction objects often exceed α.**

Under CRC (S7, SEC), 31.5–70 % of 200 fresh-pool re-splits have at least one stratum above α (Table 3), and LTT is more conservative. On the single correlated-pool split (S4, DESC, no intervals), family F1 exceeds α for three of four arms (CSF-1 0.3157, learned 0.2014, plug-in 0.1143). In F2–F4 all arms are ≤ 0.09, and only CSF-fam stays ≤ α in every family. Per family, the plug-in against CSF-fam gives −0.4441 / −0.3420 / −0.5557 / −0.7373 (F1–F4). The learned model's F1 value of 0.1102 comes at slip 0.2014, so it is not a like-for-like gain. The first-round label monotonicity gate failed (8 / 800), but the label-versus-physical check passed in all four arm × α combinations. The shrunk plug-in (S2, SEC) improves on the raw plug-in; whether the gain exceeds the SESOI is unresolved.

*Slip by friction tercile (PH, post hoc, not pre-registered).* "Marginal" here means that the bound averages over the pool's friction distribution. It promises E[slip] ≤ α for an object drawn like the calibration objects, not for an object of a given friction. Splitting the 2,800 fresh test instances into thirds by true μ (edges 0.4457 and 0.6553) at each arm's CRC point shows where the slips go (Table 3). Every arm concentrates slip on the low-friction third. The concentration is strongest for CSF-1 (0.264 in the low tercile, 0.000 in the other two) and for the learned model (0.173 / 0.062 / 0.009). The plug-in spreads slip most evenly (0.131 / 0.088 / 0.075), and the shrunk plug-in resembles the learned model (0.185 / 0.058 / 0.007). For the friction-blind baseline this is expected, since one calibrated multiplier is tight for slippery objects and generous for grippy ones. For the tactile arms it qualifies the absolute-force result of Section 5.2. The pattern is consistent with the learned model and the shrunk plug-in saving newtons mostly on slippery, high-force objects, which they hold closer to their need and which therefore slip more often than under the raw plug-in, while grippy objects get more margin. We did not test this mechanism. All four arms meet the marginal bound, and none meets α within the low-friction third (L4).

## 6 Discussion and Limitations

### 6.1 Discussion

**When the probe pays off.** In simulation, with friction independent of what the robot can see and at σ 0.2, a ramp-down probe plus a CRC-calibrated one-line plug-in lowers the excess hold force against a friction-blind conformal baseline, with pooled slip near α on average. The answer to the title question is conditional. The probe pays off in hold force when friction is not family-predictable, when the probe is quiet, and when friction varies widely. It pays off in whole-grasp force-time only for holds beyond 17.7 s (learned model) to 27.1 s (plug-in), and only *if* the certified force stays safe over such holds (L9). Outside these conditions it does not pay off. A family prior wins when friction is visible (matched D −0.58). The plug-in loses at σ 0.4 (matched −0.21), although the learned model keeps a CRC-route saving there (0.17) and beats the plug-in on the matched route (0.32). The baseline catches up as the friction range narrows. And for 1–5 s holds a grasp that skips the probe uses the least force-time.

**How large the saving is.** On every measure the probe's saving is real but modest: D 0.43 in relative excess, D_abs 0.24 in absolute excess, and 0.66 N (9.3 %) of hold force for the plug-in, or 1.01 N (14 %) for the learned model. These numbers are smaller than the relative-excess figure alone suggests, because a relative measure magnifies differences on grasps that need little force. A practitioner whose cost is actuator effort or object loading in newtons should read the absolute row. One whose cost is how far each grasp sits above its own margin should read the relative row. Either should add the fixed force-time of the probe, which neither excess measure includes.

**Which component carries the saving.** This question is unresolved across metrics, and we do not claim otherwise. On the registered relative metric at σ 0.2 and matched slip, one trained learned model adds nothing over the plug-in (C2). On the post hoc absolute metric the learned model beats the plug-in by 0.27, about as much as the probe's own gain on that metric, and a two-parameter shrinkage of the raw onset estimate recovers most of that. At σ 0.4 the learner matters on relative excess too. What the evidence does show is that the friction information enters through the probe: no friction-blind rule comes close to either probe arm on the fresh pool. How much of that information a one-line rule extracts, compared with a correction of the raw estimate, depends on the noise level and on whether newtons or relative excess are the cost.

**What the certificate means in practice.** The bound behaves as a marginal bound should. Mean slip over re-splits stays below α, but strata, one family and the low-friction third of objects carry most of the risk (L4). The online result is encouraging in one specific sense. Binary slip feedback alone is enough to pull a shifted rule back near α without replays, so the offline replay requirement (L5) need not carry over to deployment once a rule has been calibrated in simulation.

**What would change the picture.** The evidence-level gaps are those listed in Section 6.2 and in Future work: long-hold simulation at the certified force (L9), a grounded σ (L1), a hybrid prior + probe and friction-conditional calibration (L2, L4), more learned-model seeds (L10), and reproduced published controllers with a lighter protocol (L6, L7). Each of these could move one of the three conditions above. None of them is answered here.

### 6.2 Limitations

Each caveat is stated once here; earlier sections refer to it by number.

- **(L1) Simulation only; σ assumed.** One simulator, rigid contact and a modelled detector. The sign of the plug-in's saving changes across σ 0.1 → 0.4 (0.6486 → −0.2065), and σ was not grounded on hardware or on published detector error. σ is noise *added* to a modelled taxel detector whose own log friction error has SD 0.1103 at no added noise, so the total probe error is larger than the nominal σ, and its relation to a real sensor is unknown.
- **(L2) Stylised correlated pool.** One band layout, one σ and no hybrid prior + probe arm, so C4 / C5 do not show that touch is useless where a prior exists.
- **(L3) Width sweep descriptive.** One σ, no family prior, and small samples at w 0.25.
- **(L4) Marginal guarantee.** The bound holds over the pool, in expectation. 31.5–70 % of re-splits have a stratum above α, F1 exceeds α for three of four arms on the single correlated-pool split, and on the fresh pool every arm exceeds α in the lowest-friction tercile (post hoc; CSF-1 0.264, learned 0.173, plug-in 0.131), so slippery objects carry most of the risk. LTT is the conservative alternative, and no friction-conditional calibration was run.
- **(L5) Deployability.** Offline CRC needs replays at every grid force. The online route has ordering-only intervals, overshoot to 0.1389 and no physical-replay oracle.
- **(L6) Fixed high-force protocol.** The 32.7 N preload exceeds nearly every instance's F\*_mid (not all: a few need slightly more), the squeeze peak exceeds every one, and together they dominate force-time. No adaptive preload was run, so the work concerns hold force, not first-contact force.
- **(L7) Competitors.** Published controllers \cite{khamis2021real, dong2019maintaining, boonstra2024estimate, niu2026synchronized} were not reproduced; the reactive design is a negative control and was not re-designed.
- **(L8) Multiplicity.** Holm covers C1–C5 and the first-round secondaries. S1–S7, later-round and post hoc (PH) rows are unadjusted. The break-even has no interval and is a closed-form computation (Deviation 8), not a measurement. The test-slip-matched route uses test outcomes and is a frontier comparison, not a deployable certified rule.
- **(L9) Long holds not simulated.** Slip is evaluated over a 0.6 s disturbance window. The 17.74–27.07 s break-even assumes that the certified force stays safe for the whole hold, which creep, repeated disturbances or soft-contact effects could violate.
- **(L10) Known inputs and one model.** Every rule receives the true mass, the commanded acceleration and the disturbance stratum; noisy mass or an unknown stratum would change all arms. All confirmatory learned-model rows use one frozen training seed, while the first round's seed spread on the C3 contrast (0.3932–0.4494) is more than half the SESOI, so C2 is a statement about this fitted model. A probe-only protocol without the squeeze and a family prior + probe hybrid were not run. Per-family slip is reported for the correlated pool (S4) and slip by friction tercile for the fresh pool (post hoc, Section 5.4); fresh-pool per-family slip is not reported.
- **(L11) Metric dependence.** The registered metric is relative excess. The post hoc absolute-excess rows reverse the learner's role (it adds about as much as the probe), so which component "carries" the saving depends on whether the cost is relative or in newtons, and neither metric includes the probe's fixed force-time.

**Future work.** A hybrid family prior + probe rule; friction-conditional (Mondrian by μ̂) calibration, with the force cost of bringing the low-friction tercile to α; probe-noise measurement on hardware with an adaptive preload; a probe-only protocol without the squeeze; long-hold simulation at the certified force; learned-model seeds beyond seed 0; per-family (Mondrian) calibration with its force cost; and replay-free online calibration.

## 7 Conclusion

We asked when a friction-onset probe pays off for a grip whose physical slip rate is certified at α by conformal risk control. In simulation, the answer is: when friction cannot be guessed from appearance, when the probe is quiet, and when friction varies widely, and then only modestly. Against the strongest friction-blind conformal baseline, the plug-in lowers relative excess hold force (C1, matched D 0.43; C3 replicates the learned model's gain on the CRC route, 0.41). It holds 0.66 N (9.3 %) less in newtons, and the learned model 1.01 N (14 %) less (post hoc). The probe's extra phases are repaid in whole-grasp force-time only in holds beyond 17.7–27.1 s, a regime whose slip was not simulated, so any advice to probe for long holds is conditional on a long-hold test (L9). A family prior wins when friction is visible (C4, matched −0.58). The plug-in's saving reverses at σ 0.4 (matched −0.21) and as the friction range narrows. Which component carries the saving is unresolved across metrics. On relative excess at σ 0.2 the learner adds nothing over the plug-in (C2), while on absolute excess it adds about as much again (0.27, post hoc), as it does at σ 0.4 on relative excess. The bound is marginal in a concrete sense: slip concentrates on low-friction objects for every arm (L4). Online binary-feedback tracking is consistent with convergence to α. Its efficiency is inconclusive, and in the family shift tracking needs more excess force than oracle recalibration.
